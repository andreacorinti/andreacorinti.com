> *War has changed. It's not about nations, ideologies, or ethnicity anymore. It's an endless series of proxy battles fought by mercenaries and machines. War, and its consumption of life, has become a self-perpetuating system.*
>
> *(Old Snake, Metal Gear Solid 4)*

Partly out of attitude and partly out of age, I'll admit I struggle to understand the dynamics that play out among gamers younger than me.

I already know these words are going to sound very *boomer*, but, to avoid any misunderstanding, there's no criticism here of "kids these days": the goal is simply to compare the experience of Gen Z and later generations with that of us millennials.

Let's start with what's probably the single most important turning point:

the rise of online gaming.

Setting aside the very earliest *MUDs (Multi-User Dungeons)*, online gaming proper arrived in the late '90s and consolidated in the early 2000s with the arrival of broadband:

affordable and no longer tied to metered usage, the new flat-rate internet plans guaranteed the success and spread of *Ultima Online*, *Unreal Tournament*, *World of Warcraft*, and plenty of other cornerstones of online gaming.

At the same time, video games were undergoing another kind of cultural evolution, gaining early credibility and catching the interest of other media:

Hollywood, in particular, tried to cash in on their potential with mixed results (*Super Mario Bros.* in 1993 and *Street Fighter* in 1994), while still landing a few commercial hits (*Lara Croft: Tomb Raider*, 2001, and *Resident Evil*, 2002).

Money aside, this is where creatives really started to see video games as a genuine artistic and cultural phenomenon.

A telling example was the New Orleans Media Experience in 2003:

> More and more, film moguls saw games not just as a way to slap their logo exclusively onto secondary merchandise, but as a means to expand the storytelling experience. These directors had grown up with video games and had specific ideas about the creative intersections between media; they knew the most talented designers and wanted them as collaborators on their own projects. They wanted to use games to explore ideas that couldn't fit into a two-hour movie.[^48]

With the arrival of Web 2.0, video games and the world of social networks then developed a kind of mutual cross-contamination:

while social platforms borrowed the concept of *gamification* for their own interactions, video games increasingly built in features meant to plug their communities into social networks, both new (PSN, Xbox Live, Steam) and pre-existing (YouTube, Facebook, Twitch).

In a sense, this pushed gaming to "regress" back to the purely playful state that defined it at the very start, and as a result, some of the creative gains video games made in the early 2000s may have been partly lost again.

The issue is, once again, nicely captured by Hideo Kojima:

> Twenty-five years ago\*, I started adding a "story" and a "message" to video games, even though that was considered pointless at the time. And today, with the ever-growing popularity of social games, giving games depth is a trend that's fading away.[^49]  
>   
> \* *Note: the original article is from 2012, Kojima is referring to 1987, the year of the first Metal Gear*

Let's jump back in time: it's 1995, and the PlayStation crashes into our lives like a meteorite, and, for the first time, gaming becomes mainstream culture.

> "When we arrived, gaming was a niche hobby. One of our achievements was turning it into a legitimate cultural subject, right up there with music and film."

Words from Jim Ryan, CEO of Sony Computer Entertainment Europe.[^50]

Hard to argue with that:

as we've seen, within just a few years we went from video games based on movies to movies based on video games, and, right as I'm writing this, we're sailing toward whole new, unexplored cross-media territory.

For example, the gorgeous Netflix series *Arcane* gave a solid narrative backbone to the world of *League of Legends*:

launched in 2009, *League of Legends* was a prime example of that "regression" toward pure playability combined with the social features we just discussed: essentially, a game whose setting was little more than a backdrop and whose "story" was an excuse to stage pure, unfiltered gameplay.

Through *Arcane*, *League of Legends* has now undergone a deep narrative transformation — one that, however, played out in a different medium altogether: television.

Along similar lines, the Unreal Engine was used to build *The Matrix Awakens*, a video game that served as a lead-in to the film *The Matrix Resurrections*.

The Wachowski sisters, the quintessential godmothers of media convergence, have always been closely tied to the games industry:

though their reception was mixed, the older *Enter the Matrix*, *The Matrix Online*, and *The Matrix: Path of Neo* were groundbreaking experiments in cross-media storytelling for their time.

Other important cinematic crossovers come from a Japanese game studio:

Square Enix (then Squaresoft) tried the Hollywood route in 2001 with the flop *Final Fantasy: The Spirits Within*, then came back to it in 2005 with *Final Fantasy VII: Advent Children* (the crown jewel of the *"Compilation of Final Fantasy VII"* project), and again in 2016 with *Kingsglaive: Final Fantasy XV*, a prequel to the series' fifteenth mainline entry.

Alongside these artistic crossovers, the previously mentioned *esports*, *game streaming*, and other developments flourished in an increasingly fluid industry — one no longer tied to any single reference platform: by 2022, you can play the same title on PC, console, or smartphone.

As a result, it's almost impossible to find a kid in the developed world who hasn't tried at least one video game in their life.

While this scenario was largely predictable in substance, the shape it would take was fairly unforeseeable: it's as if the roots of a tree we watched being planted had grown in the strangest directions, spreading into corners we never would have expected.

This is also why some standards and preconceptions from the recent past deserve to be revisited.

Specifically, I think there's a kind of original sin that I and my whole generation of gamers are guilty of: the myth of *Longevity*.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file17.png)

> A vintage magazine review of an obscure PC game from a well-known computer gaming magazine (March 2000)

Between the '90s and the early 2000s, the score gaming magazines slapped on a game was almost always weighed against one unavoidable factor: longevity.

Basically, if a game ended quickly[^51] and didn't boast so-called *replayability*, that was generally seen as a genuine flaw, sometimes regardless of the actual nature of the game itself.

While I've always been against pure numerical scores, I'll admit that back then I bought into that criterion too, probably for purely economic reasons:

*A great game is a short game* sounded like an unbearable saying, especially applied to expensive video games, which had to earn back that steep investment in both quality and "quantity."

I think this mindset is still fairly deep-rooted today, when we think about how many hours we'll dedicate to our next gaming purchase: and yet we're looking at a completely different market!

It's an obvious point, but massive bundles of dozens of games for the price of a handful of euros put an entire back catalog within reach that's far vaster than anything available in the analog past, where even a single used title could cost a pretty penny.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file18.png)

> A vintage gaming magazine ad, December 1997

On top of that, digital bundles create a completely different kind of attachment than the one generated by physical media, which used to be the only standard (CDs, cassettes, DVDs, Blu-rays…).

Beyond the purely economic angle, there's also a whole *ritual* made of gestures and actions that we could compare to the difference between books and e-books, Spotify and vinyl records, Netflix and a movie rented from the video store:

whoever's writing this has always been a firm believer in digital for plenty of ethical and practical reasons, but at the same time, it's only fair to admit how the forced slowness of the analog world pushes you to better savor what you can actually hold in your hands.

After all, before digitization, entertainment moved at a less frenetic pace, free from today's overabundance, where everything tends to run its course pretty quickly.

A lot of *radical nostalgia* comes precisely from this: it's not that "the old stuff" was necessarily better, it's just that with less choice, there wasn't much room to be picky, so spending a lot of time appreciating and really digging into things was perfectly natural.

![Empty shelves as a video rental store closes down](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file19.png)

As I said, this same criterion applies just as easily to music, film, television, and plenty of other things, but for our purposes here we'll stick to video games:

even accounting for piracy, borrowing, and the used-game market, a video game represented a serious investment for a teenager twenty years ago.

Once bought, often after a few financial and logistical acrobatics between the store and the wallet, that kid had to make that one game last, and if the title had the nerve to actually be good, replaying it at least 3-4 times became something close to a bare minimum.

Today, it's easy, understandable, and in a sense *fair* to be picky.

> The story of every major galactic civilization tends to pass through three distinct, easily recognizable phases: Survival, Inquiry, and Sophistication, otherwise known as the How, the Why, and the Where.
>
> The first phase, for instance, is characterized by the question  
> 'How can we eat?'  
> The second, by the question  
> 'Why do we eat?'  
> And the third by the question  
> 'Where shall we have lunch?'[^52]

On the flip side, being picky, combined with the overabundance mentioned above, brings with it a vague expectation of finding lightning in a bottle every single time, maybe within the first half hour of play: being able to buy pretty much any game anywhere, at any time (often at genuinely competitive prices), the player reserves the right to be impatient.

Kojima knows a thing or two about that — with *Death Stranding*, he had to face a reception that was perhaps colder than expected:

*Death Stranding*, for whoever's writing this, is a wonderful game, but understandably not for everyone: it's slow-paced, and its narrative and gameplay are both extremely far removed from today's frenetic trends.

So it's no surprise if a player sitting on a massive backlog can't quite find the patience to give it the attention it deserves — that's completely fair.

Then there's the whole universe of guides, tutorials, and gameplay videos: rather than rack your brain over a puzzle, get lost in a map, or hunt down secrets, easter eggs, and mysteries, it's easy to lazily give in to a quick look on *YouTube*, *Twitch*, or *GameFAQs*.

This also leads to a different communal dynamic:

having fewer games and resources available also made it easier to communicate, speculate, and play with the people you actually saw in person every day, precisely because, on average, everyone was in the same boat, and alternatives were scarce.

TV with just a handful of channels, the same handful of flagship cartoons, but above all the tyrannical PlayStation, a constant presence in the afternoons of both the least and most popular kids alike:

even if a kid only got one just to play a soccer game, simply owning a PlayStation meant they'd at least vaguely know about the various *Metal Gear Solid* or *Tomb Raider* titles that filled the days of their resident nerdy classmate.

Today, thanks to modern communication tools, a much more entrenched individualism exists:

thanks to digital connectivity, a teenager into indie games might find more in common with a peer on the other side of the world than with the classmate obsessed with *Call of Duty*.

It's a phenomenon that characterizes pretty much every medium, and our society as a whole:

> If we add what's available online to what's on TV, we quickly understand why, quite rightly, we can now talk about an era of communicative abundance — an abundance that brings with it the issue of information overload
>
> \[…\]
>
> In short, an increase in supply brings with it greater ease of selection but, at the same time, produces a kind of natural fragmentation of the audience, driven by the very characteristics of the platforms themselves.[^53]

This lower degree of "individualism" and less tightly profiled set of interests also made '90s-2000s video games an interesting lab for questions of gender:

if, in its earliest days, gaming was a pretty much exclusively male world, it's in this period that girls started experiencing it in roughly the same way as their male peers.

After all, *Spyro* or *Crash Bandicoot* weren't designed with an exclusively male audience in mind, characters like Lara Croft put forward a strong, independent female role model (the polar opposite of the classic damsel in distress), and the wave of Japanese animation from that period helped build loyal audiences through the video games tied to franchises ranging from *Dragon Ball* to *Sailor Moon*.

Moving outside Sony's ecosystem, twenty-five years ago the legendary Game Boy and the *Pokémon* series were one of the most cross-cutting examples of this new gaming reality:

the Game Freak franchise is still, to this day, a phenomenon capable of reaching enormous numbers of players regardless of age, background, or gender, having found ways to present itself in different forms across multiple platforms:

we're talking about one of the most authentic examples of "media mix" (a term coined by anthropologist Mimi Ito specifically to analyze the *Pokémon* phenomenon).

Coming back to our thread, we could say that the girls of the '90s were the pioneers of what is today the well-established reality of women gamers.

The relationship between childhood and personal development matters for everyone: sticking with the same comparison to cinema, and picking back up what we said about its influence in the '30s, we can spot a similarly formative factor in modern gaming.

> Every generation has had its childhood heroes, with whom it identified and to whom it granted another life, set within the creativity of everyday play. As we grow older, cinema offers something else: it allows us to pick up a language and a style. For many, it becomes a kind of "finishing school" to attend and turn to: at the movies, you can learn the femme fatale look, pick up tips on courtship, learn how to dress.[^54]

So it's not unreasonable to think that the environmentalist themes woven into *Final Fantasy VII* (1997) planted a few seeds in the minds of countless teenagers going through adolescence, maybe nudging them toward a different relationship with the environment.

Getting to the early 2000s, we see the rise and rapid entrenchment of "trophies": PSN, Xbox Live, and Steam levels and so on would soon follow, pushing the whole conversation even further.

This creates the need to *platinum* a game, to earn proof that celebrates whatever we've been playing, to reward the player's dedication, and almost redeem them for the time they've spent.

On the flip side, without showing that kind of commitment, a player can end up with an annoying sense of incompleteness that pushes them to keep playing just to hit that goal, rather than for the pure joy of it.

The satisfaction of losing hours of your life to something repetitive and/or difficult suddenly stops being a personal indulgence, and becomes an exercise aimed at earning recognition for your efforts: a medal of valor to show off in a pointless virtual chest-thumping contest, a *status symbol* to prove to the ever-growing tribe of gamers that *no, we're not casual gamers*.  
The clear exception here is Nintendo's approach, which has always said it isn't interested in the achievement system, instead encouraging the freedom to play "however you want," without being bound by rewards that may or may not mean much.[^55]

The whole trophy question is handled well by games writer Stefano Cherubini on gameplay.cafe, who interestingly also points out the flip side of what I just described:

> These systems are nothing more than a statement of intent, a kind of moral stop sign telling you that maybe you're spending too much time on a single video game: it's time to move on to something else.
>
> \[…\]
>
> Achievements strangle the player's carefree spirit and make them lazy, presenting video games in a prepackaged format that tells us exactly how to complete a game. Everything else becomes entirely superfluous.[^56]

It's a fascinating idea, but I have some doubts about this so-called *moral stop sign*:

it's not unusual to run into trophies with absolutely zero imagination, like "chop down 1000 trees," goals that don't actually require any particular skill, but simply expect the player to sacrifice time purely for the "pleasure" of earning them.

Obviously, every video game is its own case, but it's not unusual to run into tasks that end up being more tedious than fun or stimulating for the player, so we end up with a paradox, and gaming gets stripped of what, going back to Huizinga and Caillois's concept of play, should be its defining feature:

disinterested entertainment.

Gaming, then, stops being "play" and becomes something very close to a job.

A job that's obviously unpaid, but rewarded instead through levels, trophies, and, in general, features designed to raise your status within the gaming community, along with a sense of personal accomplishment.
