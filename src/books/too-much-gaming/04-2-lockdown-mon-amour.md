> *"A memory is something that is consciously recalled, right? That's why sometimes, it can be mistaken or wrong. It's different from a memory locked deep within your heart."  
>   
> (Tifa, Final Fantasy VII)*

I think every gamer experienced the COVID lockdowns as an opportunity, in a way:

being forcibly cooped up with a ton of free time on our hands was the perfect chance to throw ourselves body and soul into one of the few things that wasn't affected by the restrictions imposed during the pandemic.

In a sense, the huge reserves of time normally taken up by everything that made up life before the virus turned into a blessing wrapped inside a curse for the more hardcore gamers among us.

A personal aside:

the grim year of 2020 gave me the time to dive into the controversial *Cyberpunk 2077* and lose myself in the gorgeous *Dark Souls III*.

It was right while I was wandering the grim lands of Lothric that I found myself lost in the dark forest of my own mind, starting to think back on the agoraphobic episodes I'd suffered through as a teenager, back in the early 2000s.

Long story short, it struck me as interesting how suddenly the whole world found itself trapped in the exact condition I had once *deliberately* retreated into.

From there, a first hunch:

I'm convinced that the lockdown so many people experienced as hell was actually *welcomed* by some, who, for once, finally had the chance to feel *normal*.

People who, incredibly, suddenly had a solid excuse to isolate themselves the way they'd always done (or at least always wanted to):

whether out of temperament or neurosis, asociality and agoraphobia are probably something we'll keep dealing with more and more in the modern world — just think of the *hikikomori* phenomenon[^14] in Japan.

As for me, long past my most intense bout of agoraphobia, armed with my "COVID excuse," I ended up sinking around sixty hours into *Dark Souls 3* in about three weeks.

Roughly speaking, that's a little under three hours of gaming a day:

I imagine that sounds like a lot to some people, but I promise you that, for the more hardcore gamers out there, it's really not much at all.

For me, counting it up and admitting it to myself was a genuine drama, given how my sense of time had shifted over the past few years, combined with the "apocalyptic" period we were living through:

those sixty hours are roughly thirty movies I could have watched, not to mention all the other things I could have spent that time on.

And, even though I loved that particular game a lot, a nasty voice started creeping into my brain:

***"What am I doing with My Life?"***

A thought that, for some reason, had a voice somewhere between a classic rock-star screaming "I Wanna Rock" and a certain Italian sitcom character's iconic yell of "Twenty years of my life!" hurled at his soul-crushing megacorp employer:

I imagine something similar must cross the minds of plenty of players about to fire up their favorite open-world RPG for the millionth time, and in fact, after talking about it with friends and acquaintances, I've confirmed that quite a few gamers deal with something similar:

a kind of *guilt.*

Taken to its most extreme form, it's a deep feeling that, starting from ordinary procrastination, feeds itself on missed personal milestones.

Realizing how, while everyone else moves forward, we've stayed trapped inside an unchanging cocoon made of anxieties, sedated by a warren of games in a kind of eternal, overtime adolescence.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file7.png)

In cases like this, it's as if video games represented an escape from time itself, not unlike the remote mountain fortress in Dino Buzzati's classic novel *The Tartar Steppe*:

dreaming of glorious battles while waiting for an enemy that never arrives, the novel's protagonist watches days, months, and years go by, always the same, unchanging as the fortress itself, in stark contrast to his peers out in the wider world who instead grow up, build careers, and start families.

He only partly realizes this, because, immersed in his own routine, he keeps feeling young right up until physical decline finally, fatally, makes him pay time's due.

For us millennials, this exact feeling has been beautifully captured by the celebrated Italian cartoonist Zerocalcare across pretty much his entire body of work, especially in his animated series *Strappare lungo i bordi (Tear Along the Dotted Line)* and his graphic novels *Macerie Prime* and *Scheletri*.

It's that realization of how the world kept moving while we stayed still, static.

That lump in the throat tends to grow with age, but if we really think about it, we've probably been carrying it around since we were kids.

To be clear: I'm talking about a kind of unease that can absolutely hit you watching a movie, reading a book, or binge-watching a TV series too. After all, if we call them pastimes, it's precisely because any hobby was born to *consume* our time.

In this sense, Guy Debord's thinking about television (from '67) still feels disarmingly relevant today:

> We know that the time-saving constantly pursued by modern society — whether in the speed of transportation or the use of instant soup — translates, for the population of the United States, into the simple fact that watching television alone takes up, on average, between three and six hours a day.[^15]

And yet, when we talk about video games, we need to point out that the scale and shape of the whole thing is completely different.

Consider the following:

watching a movie takes, on average, about two hours.

What about a book, or a TV series?

Variable timeframes, often long ones, spread out over days or weeks, but managed — and above all *perceived* — in a completely different way.

Let's take a concrete example: you can binge all of *Game of Thrones* in about 70 hours, with individual seasons averaging around ten hours each.[^16]

> The length of The Witcher 3 is based on the average reported on howlongtobeat.com

Well: video games, being the broadest and most varied entertainment medium in the world, sometimes *never actually end at all*.

Not only that: even when they do have an ending, they often carry a vague sense of being a comfortable refuge you can always return to, sometimes actively encouraged by the developer itself through features like *new game +*.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file8.png)

> A hypothetical workday for someone with a job, broken down by hours.

More often than not, a video game can turn into a kind of Platonic cave that's hard to leave and extremely easy to slip back into.

- *I'm behind on work… and yet here I am, grinding through yet another pointless random encounter in Final Fantasy.*

- *I don't know the first thing about Calculus 1, but I know the starting lineups in Football Manager by heart.*

- *I've only got a few hours a day to save a relationship, but I'd rather water my crops in Stardew Valley.*

- *My parents are getting older and the thought that they'll die someday terrifies me, but instead of spending more time with them I reinstall GTA5.*

So it's a given that most video games naturally amplify *escapism*.  
For that matter, even if you strip away the "video" and look at "play" before it ever went digital, you find that a certain need to escape reality is baked right into the DNA of play itself, as Huizinga already pointed out back in the late '30s:

> play is an action, or a voluntary activity, carried out within certain fixed limits of time and space, following a rule freely accepted \[…\] accompanied by a feeling of tension and joy, and a consciousness of "being different" from ordinary life.[^17]

In more recent times, the go-to figure for a "philosophical" framing of the medium is undoubtedly Kendall Walton, with his theory of make-believe.

Building on Walton, video-game theorist Manuel Maximilian Riolo helps us draw an important distinction between the simulative virtual and the fictional virtual:

> It's rare for an online poker player to imagine, during a hand played through their computer screen, that they're actually sitting at a real casino table with other players (often without even a face); just as it's hard for a virtual chess player to picture themselves inside a public park playing alongside other people during a computer chess session.  
> It's easier to think that these people are simply interacting with other people (in the case of *multiplayer*) or with a game program, through an electronic system made of code, bits, images, and so on.[^18]

These simulations are central to the whole notorious gambling-addiction debate, but since this book mainly deals with gaming disorder and escapism, it's naturally the more "imaginative" kind of video games we'll focus on in the coming pages.

That said, it's worth remembering that any chess simulator or video poker game is, for all intents and purposes, just as much a video game as any big-budget action-RPG: this is another point I think a lot of enthusiasts underestimate, preferring to focus only on video games in their more imaginative forms.

In the next chapters, as mentioned, we'll focus precisely on this imaginative virtual space, the foundation of virtual worlds that, with the rise of the *open-world* genre, represents the most interesting evolution of the medium.

A first observation:

every era has had people searching, more or less desperately, for an escape from reality.

But no one, before us, has ever had so many ways to escape *for real*.

> You see, I know this steak doesn't exist.  
> I know that when I put it in my mouth, the Matrix is going to tell my brain that it is juicy and delicious.  
> After nine years, you know what I've realized?  
> Ignorance is bliss.[^19]

Sorry, Morpheus, but Cypher's argument strikes me as fairly convincing, and I confess that, in Neo's shoes in *The Matrix*, I might have gone with the blue pill.

And so I can understand people who tend to feel more at home in a virtual world than in the real one.
