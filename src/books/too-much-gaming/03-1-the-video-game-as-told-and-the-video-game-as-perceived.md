*"Turn off the computer and go to sleep!"* is one of the messages that appears to the player after the ending of *The Secret of Monkey Island*.

*Monkey Island*, like many graphic adventures, can be completed *in under ten hours*.

How much do ten hours of your existence really count for?

Keep that in mind, because the whole point of what follows in these pages hinges on those five underlined words above.

As we were saying: to enjoy a milestone of video game history, you need roughly ten hours.

Well: ten hours, for a video game, is *not much*.

Careful, though!

**this book is not an attack on video games.**

If you're a politician who once fired off an angry tweet about violent video games,[^1] or belong to some association investigating the number of killers supposedly inspired by *Grand Theft Auto*, let's get this straight right away: this book is a waste of your time.

I'll say more: people who look down on video games are part of the reason I ran into some difficulties writing these pages, since I inevitably had to level some criticism at the industry itself.

Over the years, video games have been so unfairly maligned and mistreated that, as a result, the gaming community has developed a kind of overcorrection that occasionally veers into the absurd:

*Video games make us smarter.*

*Video games are good for your health.*

*Video games are the highest art form the human race has ever developed.*

*Video games are better than coffee.*

I'm exaggerating, but I promise I've seen plenty of similar headlines cross my feed, and if you're a gamer, I bet the same goes for your own "social bubble."

Nine times out of ten, articles like that get written and shared by people trying to hammer home a perfectly sacred point:

video games are *a serious thing.*

And as a result, *gaming is nothing to be ashamed of*.

I think there's a similar feeling behind the publishing world's invention of the term *graphic novel*, as if "comic book" were necessarily demeaning, synonymous with something childish and therefore not worth much consideration.

In the same way, the equation childish/juvenile product = immature and shallow also exists for video games, where it's often necessary to restate the obvious:

plenty of video games aren't designed with kids in mind at all — in fact, they're specifically age-rated accordingly.

![A phone showing an age-rating classification system for apps](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file2.png)

> It might sound obvious, and yet plenty of parents still keep ignoring official age-rating systems for games (like Europe's PEGI, https://pegi.info/)

Back to the comparison: it's interesting to notice how many traits comic books and video games actually have in common:

setting aside the far older origins of the comic book, as we've seen, we're talking about twentieth-century media inseparably tied to childhood and, as such, perpetually fighting a certain snobbish perception the wider media culture has of them.

Take, for instance, this excerpt from a lovely interview with a historian discussing his own youth:

> Back when I was in middle school, I remember buying an issue of a well-known comics magazine for the first time.
>
> \[…\]
>
> That magazine, in the '60s and '70s, played a fundamental role — alongside a handful of prominent intellectuals of the time — in introducing comics culture more broadly.
>
> That culture, which today we might no longer even call "pop" and which we now all agree to consider high culture, was back then a subject of debate and doubt among parents and psychologists dealing with kids glued to their comic books.[^2]

Basically, the flawed assumption starts from treating media aimed at children and teenagers as inherently low-value products: think of the classic bias politicians have shown toward beloved children's comic characters over the decades.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file3.png)

National newspaper headline, March 2019

I'm afraid video games, though, still haven't found their own equivalent of that kind of cultural rehabilitation — not even close.

Without underselling the important advocacy work of various academics, journalists, comic-book writers turned games commentators, and long-running specialty magazines, it has to be said that gaming media has only rarely managed to catch the attention of the wider public in a way that highlighted the cultural impact of video games.

To be clear:

this isn't a story specific to any one country — it's an international issue, tightly bound up with contemporary media culture as a whole.

Picking the comic-vs-video-game comparison back up, it's worth quoting what Hideo Kojima, the celebrated game designer behind *Metal Gear Solid* and *Death Stranding*, once observed:

> I wonder why, abroad, Japanese games aren't recognized as a specific category the way manga are... Maybe because our "TV games" are still a market where financial return still comes before everything else?  
>   
> Unlike what happened with manga, nobody has really managed to break through the discrimination and prejudice that brands our games as "products for children," bringing them fully into the world of art and philosophy.[^3]

Kojima's words lead to a broader reflection:

plenty of countries with a rich, celebrated creative tradition spanning comics, film, design, and more, have nonetheless struggled for decades to produce a globally competitive video game industry of their own.

Since it's crucial to understanding how video games get talked about in some of these places, let's try to sketch out a few reasons why (a deeper dive would honestly deserve its own separate book).

Let's start by saying that in more than a few of these countries, an aging population and a historically significant digital divide are part of the story.

Despite the widespread adoption of smartphones and the digitization of everyday tasks creating the illusion of change, computer science and programming still find far too little room outside of dedicated university departments and niche sectors, even though they now literally shape every aspect of our lives.

It's worth noting, though, that despite this technological gap, the general population in a lot of these places isn't nearly as alienated from gaming as one might expect:

as mentioned, this is fundamentally a communication problem — a matter of perception shaped by how mainstream media tells the story, but also by how gaming-specific media tells it.

We also have to reckon with yet another one of those polarizing, all-or-nothing dynamics that seem to have taken over just about everything these days.

As we've seen, while a good chunk of general-interest media keeps treating video games with a certain amount of condescension, it's not unusual to run into gaming outlets, streamers, or plain enthusiasts who swing the other way and paint video games as the pinnacle of human achievement... only to then retreat into clickbait and reviews that rarely push the conversation beyond scores, polygon counts, and framerates.

One example of this back-and-forth surfaced when a well-known actor, in an interview about a gritty crime drama he starred in, argued:

> It really seems immoral to accuse a TV show like this one of encouraging copycat behavior, when all you'd need to do is look at the video games kids actually spend their time on: games that portray nothing but dystopian futures where you have to kill, gut, and rape people, and get a thrill out of it.[^4]

The actor's comments (which he later claimed were taken out of context[^5]) sparked the usual social media firestorm, with gamers and gaming outlets reacting, followed by more or less measured responses from the actor's own fans.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file4.png)

> Growth of the video game industry's revenue over time — research by GamingScan.com, using data from Pelham Smithers and Bloomberg

It seems clear that, as long as *the world's leading entertainment industry*[^6] keeps getting treated like a yo-yo when things go well and a scapegoat when they don't, this same back-and-forth will just keep repeating itself.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file5.png)

> A major national newspaper ran the headline *"Microsoft is getting into video games"* in early 2022 — referring to the company that launched the Xbox back in 2000, more than twenty years earlier.

Add this media short-circuit to the digital divide already mentioned, and you find not just the root of the communication problem, but also one possible explanation for why some national video game industries have historically struggled to take off.

If an entire sector isn't taken seriously, it's hard to find real investment or picture a clear career path in it.

Take, for example, the leadership of a national association representing video game publishers and developers in one such country: a quick look at their résumés shows that almost all of them work for the local branches of major foreign players like Sony, Nintendo, Microsoft, EA, Ubisoft, and Blizzard.

Even accounting for a few notable homegrown exceptions (as well as an up-and-coming indie scene), the underlying point doesn't change much:

there still isn't a domestic game studio capable of making a real dent on the international stage.

This exposes a kind of vicious circle, and maybe explains why the video game still hasn't been fully embraced and legitimized among the media the way comic books have (at least partly) managed to be.

That said, it's worth repeating that the medium's perceived credibility is still very much a work in progress everywhere else too: at the end of the day, this dynamic isn't wildly different from what you'd find even in the United States.

But let's set the US and Japan aside for a moment and stick to Europe:

besides the French giant Ubisoft, one of the most industrially interesting examples is Poland's CD Projekt Red which, with *The Witcher* series, the more recent *Cyberpunk 2077*, and the GOG.com distribution platform, has become one of the central companies in the entire Polish economy:

the company is worth more than the country's leading bank[^7] and has for years been unanimously recognized as one of the most important game studios in the world.

Moving past the economics, it's worth looking at the politics too: in the early 2000s, the US Army launched *America's Army*, a first-person shooter with clear propaganda intentions and the ambition to embed itself in youth culture of the period.[^8]

Along similar lines, a BBC investigation showed how certain far-right circles have exploited online game chats to recruit new members,[^9] and it's well known how memes and virtual spaces closely tied to gaming contributed to the spread of the alt-right phenomenon during the 2010s.[^10]

All in all, without getting into "highbrow" reflections on ludology and narratology, one thing is certain:

underestimating video games by downplaying their influence is a mistake.

Gaming has, for decades now, made up a fundamental slice of our culture, of how new generations grow up, and of how we spend our money.

Fortunately, the general public seems to understand this far better than mass media and politics do, as shown by a recent national survey on the domestic gaming sector:

> **This national sector employs around 1,600 people across 160 companies** (79% of them under age 36) and generates roughly €90 million in revenue.  
>   
> \[…\]  
>   
> **The public clearly believes in the sector's potential.**  
> 59.4% think the industry is capable of generating plenty of **new jobs**, especially for young people.  
> **57.9%** **think the talent and creativity of local developers can turn gaming into a genuine ambassador for homegrown creative industries abroad**. 54.2% (rising to 58.9% among college graduates) think growth in the sector contributes to the broader economic recovery.[^11]

In partial defense of all this bias and these communication issues, it's worth considering that we're dealing with an extremely new and complex subject of study, because when we say *video game*, we're actually talking about a whole lot of very different things.

Take a standard dictionary definition:

> An electronic device that allows you to play by interacting with images on a screen.

And another, more detailed one:

> Software that, through sophisticated graphics, simulates recreational scenarios (sports competitions, combat, or various challenges set in the most diverse locations), allowing one or more players to play against each other or against the computer; it can run on a dedicated electronic device, like those found in arcades, or on a personal computer.

Bottom line?

Under this one label, you'll find *absolutely everything*.

It's as if we were talking generically about "food," while lumping together things as different from one another as eggplant, tofu, or tiramisu.

In the same way, when we talk about "video games" as a category, we might be talking about works of art, esports, simple pastimes, or straight-up gambling.

This problem has been analyzed quite well by media scholars in the field:

> the term remains, even today, inevitably tied — at the level of collective perception — to something childish, self-contained, unproductive, and therefore, one might conclude, ill-suited to highlighting the deeper qualities of the video game as a medium.[^12]

The lack of better definitions is fairly normal if you consider how quickly it's all evolved relative to its young history.

Born in the United States in the years after World War II, it wasn't until the '70s (the era of *Pong*) that video games saw their first real boom, reaching full maturity in the '80s and '90s, and then exploding in the new millennium thanks to the internet finally going mainstream.

At the same time, the small tribe of gamers grew exponentially with every passing generation: gaming is inseparable from a remarkable communal aspect — that of *gamers* (a term I'll keep using throughout this book[^13]), a community among the most prominent players in the vast *fan culture* mapped out by Henry Jenkins in his book *Convergence Culture*.

Gamers of the early 2000s were in fact among the main pioneers and champions of the modern concept of *fandom*, which, with the rise of social networks, would go on to become a cornerstone of all contemporary entertainment: back in the early days of web communities (forums, chat rooms, blogs), gamers established themselves as the leading producers of *grassroots* content — amateur material, made "from below" and disconnected from corporate logic.

Through *fan art*, *fan fiction*, and especially *mods* (modifications of commercial video games made by ordinary fans), whole scenes emerged so vast and long-lived that they often ended up standing entirely on their own, independent of the original game they were based on — a phenomenon I consider similar to the *Do It Yourself* ethic of punk culture: to name one striking example, the global hits *Dota 2* and *League of Legends* both trace back to *Defense of the Ancients*, a custom "map" for *Warcraft III* created by a few fans of Blizzard's game.

Creativity and industry aside, consider how crucial this communal aspect was in forming friendships and bonds between the '80s and '90s:

personally, thanks to video games I met important people, both in the virtual world and the real one (which have often ended up blending into each other), cultivated several interests, and, through roundabout paths, developed skills that have proven very useful both professionally and creatively.

This is also why I want to stress again that I would never dream of demonizing this universe of pixels I'm happy to call home. If it weren't such an awkward title, this little book could have been called *Play Responsibly*.

*Too Much Gaming!* is, first and foremost, a reflection on how we spend our *life's time* playing, and, second, an invitation toward moderation.

Aiming for a good glass of fine wine, while trying to avoid one too many bottles of vinegar.
