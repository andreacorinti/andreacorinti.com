> *Fausto Tritapepe, born in 1986, is a philosopher and programmer who loves video games. He's lived and worked in Glasgow, Scotland since 2016.*

Interview conducted on 11/23/2021

**Hi Fausto! Can you give me a quick rundown of your background and tell me what you do these days?**

Hi! I've got a double degree — one in philosophy, from ten years ago now, so that's a fairly old one…

**Well, philosophy doesn't exactly expire!**

It doesn't expire, but let's say I'm not quite the Kant I used to be!

**At least you didn't turn into some armchair philosopher-politician…**

(laughs) No, I didn't turn into that!

I've lived in Scotland for five years now and I'm currently studying Computer Science.

I'm a developer focused on object-oriented programming, and I've also been getting into web development — it's not exactly my world, but I've been putting time into it since JavaScript is basically daily bread at this point, and it matters more and more across different fields.

**Couldn't agree more — on that note, it surprises me that the subject isn't taught more intensively in school from early childhood, or at least isn't left entirely to specific university departments.**

Here in the UK, some of that's already happening: there are elementary school kids learning Python, for instance *(editor's note: a programming language)*, and I think that's true for most countries with a more open, forward-looking market.

**I'm afraid where I come from there's a real gap to close on that front, which I think partly explains the state of the local video game industry.**

**On that note, let's get into it: can you remember what your first video game was, or your first real encounter with this whole world?**

Going way back: I started around four or five, playing on two old consoles with my older brother, one of those early 8-bit home computers and an Atari. I don't remember the exact model names, but the first games I played were old titles like *Space Invaders*, *Arkanoid*… then I'd say I got into gaming a bit more "seriously" at the arcades:

*Mortal Kombat*, *Street Fighter I and II*, *Captain Commando*, and the arcade version of *Mario Bros*… I'm pretty "boomer" in that sense!

**Same here! On average, how many hours a week do you play?**

According to Steam, it's around 20 hours right now: gaming is still a passion of mine, but it obviously depends a lot on the period.

**Let's say, hypothetically, that starting tomorrow you had a lot more free time — how much do you think that number would go up?**

Considering I'd probably spend most of my free time studying programming, I'd guess… 10-15 hours? It really depends on the game too, obviously.

**Favorite genres?**

I don't really have fixed genres. Over time I've experimented and discovered new things, but I'd say I remain a fan of old-school RPGs like the various *Baldur's Gate* games, *Vampire the Masquerade: Bloodlines*… though lately I've been really enjoying open-world games, so maybe calling it "RPGs" in the strict sense is wrong:

I mean, more broadly, games that offer freedom, with customization options and so on.

I also like fighting games — beat 'em ups.

**Which, as you were just saying, is kind of where you started out.**

Yeah, though it was a bit of an accident, to be honest: fighting games were mostly just what you'd find in arcades back then.

**Can confirm — and with not much choice available, there wasn't much room to be picky. Often the real fight was between kids arguing over turns at the *Street Fighter II* cabinet!**

(laughs) Yeah, exactly. I also like action and adventure games… games with an interesting story behind them, basically, with a plot that really pulls you in.

**Good segue into some questions for the philosopher: in the book I talk repeatedly about escapism, the tendency to retreat into a comfort zone that lets you forget about the real world, avoid dealing with the hard parts of actual life.**

**Obviously the causes behind that feeling are complicated and it can apply to plenty of things (from Netflix to social media), but don't you think certain video games can amplify it quite a bit? I'm thinking mostly of younger players, but it applies to gamers in general too.**

That's a specific, complicated question, and it needs a complicated answer… from my point of view, the answer has several layers. First, there's a generational angle:

when it was born, gaming was seen as "The New Thing" — certainly a new form of entertainment, but *also* a new way to meet people: going to arcades meant running into people, because it was the trend of the moment, even if it was still a niche phenomenon compared to today.

That's the same dynamic social media has today, with today's teenagers experiencing that same sense of novelty, but with a negative twist: total physical disconnection from other people.

Meaning, you tend to shut yourself behind your phone or your computer because you still have the means to stay in touch with others without actually being physically there with them.

These days gaming is mainstream, so it's an almost mandatory presence in young people's lives, and of course some developers take advantage of that by building gambling-like mechanics right into their games.

**In one chapter of the book, I look at how it's practically impossible today to find a teenager who's never tried at least one video game in their life: it's become more than just a cultural fad the way it was twenty years ago, don't you think?**

More than that. The thing is, gaming used to be separate from how we related to other people; today, gaming and social media move together: *gaming has become social media, and social media has become gaming*. Take the PlayStation Network, linked to your Facebook, Twitch, Twitter account… Today, social media, which is a way to communicate, has merged with gaming, which is a way to entertain yourself… so, while the old logic used to be *"I go to the arcade and meet up with friends I'll later go out with,"* today it's *"I log into the PlayStation Network and connect with my friends through social media at the same time."* It's the same thing, just no longer made up of people meeting in person, but of using digital tools instead.

**So you could say a person can keep their social-animal side intact while effectively staying locked in their own cave, without ever having to deal with the challenges of moving through the outside world. All of that became a lot more obvious after COVID.**

And here's where the negative side comes in: we are, like you said, social animals. We're creatures with feelings, with sensations, who need to "feel," to physically perceive what's around us, especially other people.

The thing is, we've become so dependent on these tools that we're now completely disconnected — we no longer see other people as "beings like us," but as letters on a screen, as artificial intelligences.

Applying that to gaming, we stop seeing people as figures who share our same needs, fears, and so on, and start seeing them as forms of entertainment instead.

On Facebook, for instance, we don't actually care about "the person," just about what they've posted — their content.

**And in turn, this image made up of all these bits of "content" we put out ends up mattering more than the real whole person.**

Exactly. Coming back to video games, there's a kind of manipulation at play from whoever built these tools: developers are inevitably tied to the consumerist logic of the contemporary world:

we spend money to get something that entertains us and makes us feel good for a moment, but there are genuine "coercive" techniques out there designed to keep the player tied to the game.

**In the book, putting it in fairly extreme terms, I actually talk about "developers as pushers" — a phenomenon that maybe gets underestimated in the industry, don't you think?**

There's still some uncertainty about the relationship between gaming and certain phenomena. But, as I've seen firsthand and you surely know too, modern video games have things like "loot boxes," which are basically straight-up gambling.

**And at that point we've left gaming disorder behind and stepped fully into gambling addiction.**

Right, and keep in mind we're talking about a sneaky kind of gambling, because you still "*win*" something every single time.

There's a behavioral psychology angle here too:

you get used to always receiving a reward, because buying a loot box guarantees you some content, even if it's not what you were actually after. On top of that, in some cases, the more times you try, the higher the odds get that the rare item will actually drop.

**That's interesting, I'd honestly never thought about it that way: in this mechanic, *you never lose*. So it's not really a slot machine in the strict sense, because even if you don't get what you want, you still win something else, and your odds of getting what you're after next time go up.**

Exactly: you never lose, and knowing your odds keep improving pushes you to keep going. Plus, the rules are usually spelled out clearly:

if, say, the base odds of getting item X are 20%, you already know that after your next failed attempt they'll go up to 30% or 40%, so sooner or later item X will drop.

Basically, you're already a victim of the system, because you've spent money on something you could normally get just by playing the game.

The thing is, you end up spending five, ten times more to get something that isn't actually worth your money, but it becomes important to you because it's turned into a scratch-card-style challenge, and that's where the pathology kicks in, because that goal ties you into a gambling-style mechanic.

And it's not even total, pure gambling, where you play and genuinely don't know if you'll win: there's always a consolation prize.

**So do you think loot boxes could end up being worse than other forms of gambling?**

Yes, because as I said, even if you don't find what you want, you still get some small reward, and at the same time you're "tortured" by that unmet goal: you're left with a relentless need to keep going.

On top of that, there's no real limit, because loot boxes keep getting constantly updated.

**That brings me back to how video games get talked about back where I'm from, how rarely certain topics get properly addressed in our media, with a lot of confusion between gaming disorder and gambling addiction. You've lived in Scotland for years now: do you notice a different perception of gaming compared to back home? Is it a more accepted world over there?**

Given that I haven't lived back home for five years, so my sense of how the sector was seen is a bit dated, but overall? I'd say no, there aren't huge differences. Maybe here in the UK there's more attention paid to gaming "fever," which gets framed as "a drug to stop thinking."

**That actually reminds me of a common mix-up back home: as I mentioned, gambling addiction is specifically tied to gambling, while gaming disorder, or video game addiction, is something completely different that maybe isn't well understood there, and the wrong term often gets used for it.**

Here in the UK it gets treated as a genuine disorder — I'd say the underlying culture is different, but we've got kids getting bombarded with Japanese anime, heavily influenced by trends and so on: from what I can see, we've got kids feeling more and more sad and depressed, and finding in gaming a companion, something that makes them feel good and lets them forget about family problems, life problems… and not think about their own responsibilities.

I'd say kids don't really have the awareness of "living badly," or the ability to coexist with life's hardships.

A video game puts you up against obstacles like a brutal boss fight in a hardcore action-RPG — an obstacle that can be hard to get past, that makes you angry, but that always has an easy solution behind it:

you can try and try again as many times as you want, look up a video guide that teaches you a way to make things even easier, and so on.

Real-life problems, obviously, are a whole different story — there are no guides, and even though we do get some attempts, they're generally limited.

**Tying that back to the anime point you made, we could say there's a "meritocratic" element in gaming similar to what we see in a lot of shonen battle anime: the idea that if you train, put in the effort, and work hard, you'll eventually beat the boss, while in real life, no matter how hard we try, things obviously aren't that simple or linear.**

No, exactly: sure, thankfully effort does get rewarded sometimes, but there's a whole mess of factors in between — like socioeconomic circumstances (plus sheer, unknowable luck) — that always end up dominating the real world. There are no real rules, and the neat, structured nature of a video game becomes, in that sense, a way to escape reality.

Maybe those of us who are a bit older can experience it with a different kind of awareness, because we've lived through a world both without video games and after them, whereas young people, having been born straight into this reality, are confused, because their reality *is* that: gaming, anime, social media — their whole world is often concentrated right there.

**Pretty worrying scenario put that way, but completely understandable.**

**Sticking with generational differences, you've probably noticed how nostalgia is a recurring theme throughout pop culture, and video games are no exception:**

**the phenomenon obviously makes sense from the producers' side (at the end of the day, you're selling a famous title that basically markets itself), but how do you explain the eternal commercial success on the buyers' side?**

**To give another current example, the tenth-anniversary edition of a hugely popular fantasy RPG shot straight to the top of Steam's sales charts within days: so why do we keep buying games we already own?**

Correct me if I'm wrong here, but I think the most intense version of this trend started with the remake of *Final Fantasy VII*, or really with its announcement…

**Right, shortly after *Advent Children* (2005) came out, a technical tech demo for PlayStation 3 was shown, but people were already talking about it at least fifteen years ago, maybe even earlier.**

You have to consider that, beyond wanting to sell to us *boomers*, developers also want to reach younger players intrigued by a title's legendary status, because new gamers have heard about these games without ever being able to actually play them (except through emulators), so it's a way to hook them too, by presenting products that meet today's standards for visuals, framerate, resolution, and so on.

**Makes sense, but then how do you explain "us old-timers" buying these games?**

I don't want to get too "mystical" about it, but I'd say it's an attempt to relive old emotions.

If you think about it, even just hearing the name *Final Fantasy VII* sends us right back in time.

**So we want to buy back our childhood.**

It's like we want to *relive* our childhood.

It's also pretty much a given that a lot of us in our thirties and forties feel the weight of our generational situation — we feel out of step with what should be our own moment:

we're people who no longer feel fully part of the world, because everything's faster now, everything changes so suddenly.

And this sense of disorientation, which started with us, got passed straight down to the generations that basically came into a world where it already existed, while we hadn't even finished working through it ourselves.

In that sense, hearing old names tied to our childhood again, wanting to relive old glories… and that phrase, *old glories*, really says it all: getting to experience again something that was a triumph, a source of real satisfaction for us — we like that, and we want it.

And there's marketing psychology at play there too: if you tell me *"they're remaking Metal Gear Solid,"* I immediately think back to the twenty thousand times I played it back in the day, already knowing that picking it up again now wouldn't give me the same feelings, because I've already used it up. It's *an experience* that's already been consumed.

The remake, on the other hand, is something familiar, and therefore exciting for the reasons we just talked about, but also *new*!

**So I get to relive my childhood emotions, but at the same time I'm not really eating the same reheated soup — I'm getting a whole new dish.**

A dish that's obviously inspired by the same soup, but that at least offers a recipe reworked by a new chef, with a new way of cooking.

That's why we're curious to see what *Metal Gear Solid*, *Final Fantasy VII*, or *Resident Evil* would look like today… we already know we love the originals, but we're curious to see what they'd be like now, taking advantage of today's technology.

**Really interesting, personally I relate a lot to that curiosity angle, though I'll admit I still have some vague doubts about how widespread it really is.**

**Changing topic — since, like me, you're someone living abroad from your home country, have you noticed how video games act as a kind of cosmopolitan flag? There are differences, but from one city to the next, the flagship titles in each genre are almost always the same everywhere. Do you think this "globalization" is a problem or an advantage?**

That's a tough question. It's an advantage because information has, in a sense, become standardized, so it's easier to communicate and everyone more or less picks up on the same thing: your message stays identical no matter where you are.

But it's also true that how we interpret things stops being subjective — we're turning everything "objective": the same game is the best-seller in one city just as it is in another, because even if two people in those two places live in completely different cultures and realities, since the whole world says a certain game is amazing, that "greatness" ends up getting imposed in a way: everyone plays the same thing, and if you push back because you don't like it, you come across as out of line — *you're not normal, you don't know how to judge things properly*. And that's part of where bullying dynamics come in among kids too...

**Speaking of which, digging into the *Fortnite* phenomenon, I found out that in some circles, if you're 12-13 and you don't play it, you're basically cut off from entire social groups.**

It's kind of the old fashion-world logic: the story goes that if you don't want to wear a certain luxury designer item, it's not because you don't like it, it's because you're poor.

In the same way, if you want to be relevant in certain circles, you *have to* play *Fortnite*, you *have to* play *League of Legends*, you *have to* play whatever's trendy.

Trying to re-educate kids out of that doesn't really make sense, since whoever's supposed to do the re-educating is a victim of the exact same dynamics.

It's hard to grasp that you don't have to conform to be accepted, that you don't have to be a copy of someone else.

**I guess it's just yet another symptom of a broader problem with society, one that maybe just shows up more clearly in video games.**

Well, gaming is a reflection of society after all. And then society ends up reflecting the game itself right back, because young people learn things there, meet other people there (going back to that whole gaming-social media overlap), and so fiction turns into reality.

**Let's do a time jump: it's 2031 and we're a couple of cheerful forty-somethings. What are video games like?**

In 2031, given how much everyone's talking about artificial intelligence these days, I honestly don't know.

We'll probably have video games produced by machines — creativity dictated by an algorithm, no longer spontaneous but mathematical.

Probably everything will get a little sterile, a little uniform.

**So a scenario where I feed an AI the words "RPG," "Action," "Cyberpunk," and it procedurally generates the whole game design, more or less like what already happens with procedurally generated levels in a lot of games.**

I'll say I see it happening across the board in a lot of creative fields, music included: everything turns into a copy of something else, because that's the safe bet. I think by 2031 we'll start seeing something like that. Maybe it won't be the norm, but I think that's roughly the direction we're heading in.

**So you don't see an "auteur" future for the industry? I'm thinking simply of someone like Hideo Kojima, a kind of Martin Scorsese of video games: you don't see a scenario where other developers reach his level, stamping their own clear artistic signature on things?**

There can be, there will always be exceptions like that, but they'll increasingly struggle to produce something genuinely new, I think.

A figure like Kojima will always manage to pull something off, and yet even a beautiful game like *Death Stranding*, while being something new, still carries a lot of *Metal Gear* baggage with it, and in a way that's inevitable, because it's his signature — at the end of the day, Kojima *is* Metal Gear.

**We really are talking about one of the most important auteurs in gaming history, and yet most of his work is concentrated in that one single series, which, in a way, has also kept him a bit of a prisoner over the years.**

**Production demands have forced him to keep singing the same song, in a sense.**

Yeah. Obviously I can't predict the future, but given how things are evolving, and how fast, I have this feeling: we're going to keep moving toward automating things, producing more with less effort. And what's the simplest way to do that? Use a machine.

**Sort of the end of craftsmanship and the rise of the assembly line, put that way.**

**I was thinking that, actually, in something like a football or sports management sim, beyond the obvious database updates for player rosters, what it really comes down to is replicating physics and photorealism more and more faithfully — we're not talking about games that need a huge amount of creative leaps (at least on paper), so it's not unreasonable to think that in the future a solid 70% of development could get handled through automation.**

That's about right, we'll see.

**Last question:**

**your all-time favorite game, and you only get one answer!**

That's a hard one!

Can I name a whole series instead?

I'd say *Legacy of Kain*, but if I have to pick one specific game, *Soul Reaver*.

**Great choice, thanks so much for this interview!**

Thank you, talk soon!
