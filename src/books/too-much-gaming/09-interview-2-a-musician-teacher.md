> *Annalisa Meloni, born in 1986, is a musician and teacher: clarinetist with a chamber orchestra, she teaches and works with a couple of local music schools.*

Interview conducted on 11/25/2021

**Hi Annalisa! Let's start with a quick professional background: can you sum up (if you can, it's a pretty long résumé!) your educational path and what you do these days?**

Hi! So:

- High school diploma with a social sciences focus

- Clarinet diploma from a music conservatory (old-style program)

- Bachelor's degree in Cultural Heritage Science and Technology

- Two-year advanced clarinet program at another conservatory

- Teaching certifications for music at middle and high school level

and last year I finished a saxophone qualification too.

Right now I'm a permanent music-instrument teacher at a middle school, and I run a bunch of civic-education projects — for instance, I'm in charge of the local youth council program.

On top of that, I teach instrument lessons, orchestra conducting, and early music education for younger kids, adult music education at a couple of private schools, and I also play in an orchestra.

**Well, your days must be pretty packed! For this interview I'm especially interested in your role as a teacher: I'm really curious how video games get perceived by kids today.**

In my case my students range from age three to eighty, so we're covered on that front!

**I bet! But let's start by talking about *your* relationship with video games: do you remember what your first one was? Your first console, or your first encounter with this whole world?**

My first encounter with video games goes back to MS-DOS…

**So you were born a *PC gamer*!**

Yeah, absolutely computer! I had this little program called "Numbers": it had animals drawn in DOS. From there I went further, and around age 4-5, I played *Lemmings, Prince of Persia*…

**The good old PC classics from the '90s, basically.**

Exactly!

**And your favorite genres? What were they, what are they now, have they changed over time…**

I'd say, having kept some consistency and having gotten to try a lot of things over the years, my favorite genre is still the classic turn-based JRPG, followed by point-and-click adventures.

**Final Fantasy, Monkey Island, and the like.**

Exactly, though my actual baptism by fire with point-and-click was with a super-niche game called *Opera Fatal*, a murder mystery set in an opera house. Then, of course, there's the classic solitaire, minesweeper…

**The usual office time-killers!**

Exactly, but the gaming experiences that mattered most to me, like I said, are the JRPGs.

**Given how packed your schedule is, how much time do you spend gaming on average? Let's say a rough estimate of hours per week.**

Lately a weekly estimate isn't really doable: I manage two or three hours a month.

**Two or three hours a month? That little?**

Yeah, my work schedule doesn't allow for much more. Then, sure, there are periods when I manage to play more — summer, when I work less, vacation time… in those cases I might even play two or three hours a day.

Keep in mind I bought myself *Civilization VI* because it was on sale, but I already know I won't get to try it before December 8th.

**I can relate, in my own small way: I definitely have more free time than you do, but I often buy games I don't even have time to install.**

**But let's say, hypothetically, you suddenly had more free time — how much would your gaming hours go up?**

Oh, well, I'd get to *at least* three hours a day! Maybe more, but I've noticed lately that I can only really play "well" for a couple hours at most before I start getting tired: I can't pull the marathon sessions I used to as a kid.

**I'm sure a packed schedule plays into that too. And how much does the platform matter to you? Console, PC, smartphone…**

It depends on the game I'm playing: I definitely feel all the limits of smartphones, I don't think they can offer as complete a gaming experience as a PC or console.

Right now I mostly play on PlayStation… oh, but I almost forgot about my tablet! I use that a lot for rhythm games: I'm playing pretty much every Rayark title out there, I love them both for the genre and for the music choices.

**That leads me to one of the questions I had in mind: given your work in teaching and music, how much do you think video games fit into your job? I've always found it interesting how "to play" in English covers both playing games and playing an instrument.**

**I'm thinking simply of games like *Rocksmith*, but also how it applies to studying in general: I see it a lot in my own work too, since I'm involved in teaching as well. How do you see it?**

I've noticed that connecting the two is really interesting from a teaching standpoint, because for kids, gaming is a comfortable environment, which really matters when you're teaching.

When I used to teach music, I actually ran a lot of quizzes through an online platform called *Kahoot!*

**I get it, I work with a platform that has similar systems, roughly speaking.**

Let's say it's a multiple-choice quiz system, where you've got two devices: one that projects the questions, and one you use to answer through colored buttons. It works both remotely and in a computer lab, there are competitions, response times, scores, even little *quests*.

**I can confirm that, even though I work in a technical field, this kind of system really seems to pay off. But talking specifically about the musical side of things — where something like *Rocksmith* hands you an instrument with the idea of "playing" it — what's your take? Do you think it's just fun messing around for its own sake, or can games like that seriously help from a teaching standpoint too?**

Keep in mind kids are mostly glued to their phones, so honestly I've recommended those Rayark titles I mentioned, which are pretty similar in concept to *Guitar Hero*, or the various *Just Dance* games too: they're all pretty useful for rhythm. Students get really into it, and it's a way to help them get better at staying on beat, even if we're not talking about especially precise software.

Paradoxically, there are flashier tools out there to get them genuinely interested in playing an instrument, but studying an instrument is always hard work and requires constant practice… it's not really something playful, so to speak.

Technology gets used for a lot of other things, but for the "instrument" discipline it doesn't help all that much. For the "music" discipline, though, it does.

**So we could say video games are really useful for theory, but the practical side still works the old-fashioned way in the end.**

Yeah, hands-on instrument practice can't be replaced. Software helps with other aspects: timing, reading notes, and so on… but hands-on practice with the instrument itself is irreplaceable.

Sure, if you put them in front of an EWI they have fun, but that's different. I don't know if you know what that is…

**Nope, you've got me there!**

It's an actual wind instrument — hook it up to an audio interface (over Bluetooth these days) and you can make it produce any kind of sound.

**Ah, so it's like a MIDI keyboard in a sense: you hook it up to a PC and, through software, give it whatever "voice" you want.**

Exactly, but unlike a keyboard, it's a real instrument, so you control certain modulations and dynamics yourself, because you're actually blowing into it.

**Interesting, now I want one!**

**Stepping outside the music world for a second — I'd love to get your perspective as a woman for another angle on gaming: today, women gamers are a well-established reality, but until a few years ago the industry seemed like a kind of male monopoly, maybe because of a distortion in how gaming gets talked about. From your experience, how many women gamers do you know?**

Very few, actually, when I tell my female colleagues (or women my age in general) that I love video games, they always give me a weird look *(laughs)* and call me a tomboy, so I think the bias is still pretty deep-rooted, at least outside of younger people.

**It really is tricky to figure out where we stand on this, since it always comes back to the gap between perception and how it gets told: for instance, when we were kids, I remember magazines and the first gaming websites all being aimed squarely at a male audience…**

I remember, I remember.

**…but today, based on who I know, from Twitch to any big comics-and-gaming convention, I've run into plenty of women gamers who, like me, grew up in the PlayStation and Game Boy era: a small undercurrent that existed back then, even if it never got told anywhere near the right way, and that maybe stayed a bit of a hidden pocket precisely because it had so little space in gaming media, right?**

Sort of, yeah, but I think there's something to this handover between generations: even today, women gamers haven't exactly been fully accepted either.

Listening to educators, or parents themselves, there's still a tendency to stigmatize a girl who spends a lot of time on video games. What's changed, I think, is really the range of content available, if you'll let me put it that way: take *Genshin Impact*, it's built as a product designed to appeal to a female audience… compare it, say, to what something like the old *World of Warcraft* offered.

**But are you sure *Genshin Impact*, with its aesthetic, is actually aimed at girls, and not more at fan-service for a male audience? You know, dressing up the anime girl in certain skimpy outfits and so on.**

No, well, I've got several middle-school kids who play *Genshin Impact* and show me screenshots specifically because they know their teacher plays it too…

**…and I bet they're thrilled about it!**

Yeah, they're absolutely delighted to have a *nerdy teacher!* Anyway, so, even though there's sure to be a good chunk of creeps out there too, I can tell you the girls have a blast with it: they all play the female character, while the boys tend to switch back and forth… but that level of attention to accessories, outfit colors, weapon colors, showing up for an event just to get a cute accessory — a boy generally doesn't have that.

**So in those elements you find that the developer specifically had a female audience in mind.**

I think so, yeah, it's really obvious to me in *Genshin Impact*. For example, in something like *Final Fantasy XIII*, we've got a series of games where female protagonists take center stage, but you can tell they're built with a male audience's attention in mind, while in newer generations of games I'm seeing titles that are more accessible and attentive to what a female audience actually wants.

**Can you give an example of what these younger generations actually play, and whether you notice substantial differences between boys and girls?**

I'll specify that, as long as it's in a controlled setting, families get very involved in choosing video games… so for boys, parents tend to go with *Minecraft*, while for girls they lean a bit more toward games you play together as a family (Nintendo Wii style, let's say), but also the classic *Super Mario* on Switch. Once kids grow up and start having their own phone, spending time alone, and living more independently, that's when they start making their own individual choices.

**So it's around preadolescence that they start playing whatever they want instead of whatever gets "imposed" on them by their families?**

Exactly: I see plenty of high-school girls who play *Minecraft* regularly, for instance.

**So you're confirming that there's a solid group of women gamers among younger generations.**

Yes, definitely.

**Sticking with that, have you noticed how gaming has become a kind of status symbol for a lot of kids? Not playing a certain title risks making them feel "cut off": have you seen anything like that among your students, or in your day-to-day life?**

From what I've seen, the type of game varies a lot depending on trends: for both younger and older kids, it's owning the console that determines who gets cut out.

Take a game like *Fortnite*, for instance, where kids talk in chat: if everyone's playing on PS4 and one kid doesn't have it, they get left out.

**So you're saying if a whole class plays on PlayStation and one kid ends up with an Xbox, that kid gets "discriminated against"?**

Pretty much, yeah, or you end up at a friend's house who has the top-of-the-line console:

at that point, whoever owns the device becomes the leader, because having the PlayStation means holding the power of *"I'll invite you — I won't invite you."* It happened a ton at my old school: right when the PS4 had just come out, the kid who'd just gotten one acted exactly like that.

**Something pretty similar happened back in our day too.**

Exactly, but honestly I don't think kids have changed at all.

**I basically think the same, the thing is everything else has changed quite a bit, especially communication tools:**

**internet access, social media, the fact that they can satisfy their need for social connection digitally without ever leaving the house…**

**So, even though kids have the same needs, feelings, and tantrums, today there are new technological tools that have changed the way they experience all of it, right?**

But keep in mind this only kicks in once they're a bit older: in elementary school and the first years of middle school, they still prefer meeting up in person, because that's genuinely a need at that age, it's what their age calls for.

**So you're saying it's with adolescence that everything else shows up?**

After eleven for girls and twelve for boys, I'd say.

But for them, the real video game is *TikTok*, where they basically do the same things people used to do playing *The Sims*. You could say they genuinely build identities through social media and apps, and in the last few post-pandemic months, the height of fun for them has been making *TikTok* videos following whatever trend is going around.

**So it's like TikTok has gone from being social media to being a video game, or rather, the kids themselves have become the game, being both the players and the *content* at the same time. Really interesting.**

Exactly, it's the same with Instagram: it's like everything turns into one giant role-playing game. They track views and followers like they're levels… nowadays what they aspire to is becoming a YouTuber or influencer, so they upload stuff obsessively.

For example, when I tell my students I have a YouTube channel, they ask me, *"Miss, how many followers does it have? How many views?"* and I explain that I'm not marketing my videos, I'm not making content to reach a global audience or boost my stats...

**There's a kind of *gamification* there too, isn't there?**

Exactly: the whole logic of gaming, through technology, has spread to social media too. In fact, what we see as teachers (and what we work on through a whole range of interventions) is precisely the fact that kids don't realize that *social media isn't a video game*.

Kids tend to treat it exactly the same way, as if it were something detached from reality: they don't realize we're talking about platforms where they're handing over their own sensitive data.

**Coming back to video games proper, but staying on the topic of gamification, how much do you think video games influence society, politics, and today's media? Years ago I remember a politician giving an adventurous campaign speech inside *Second Life*, and more recently another one publicly criticized video games…**

**Like we said, video games influence everything, but my sense is that outside of the internet and gaming-specific outlets, they still haven't really found their place, especially compared to film and comics, which often do show up in the pages of major mainstream newspapers and magazines.**

**Why do you think that is?**

Because they're still seen as an alienating product.

Personally I think there are far worse things than video games, at least when they're experienced in a healthy, playful way, but in the media they're still treated as a complete waste of time.

Even the Japanese figure of the *otaku*, for instance, is still deeply stigmatized.

I mean, we're talking about people who spend their time enjoyably pursuing a passion, but who, from a certain point of view, get stigmatized if that passion doesn't end up "producing" something.

Obviously someone who binge-watches TV shows nonstop also gets seen as wasting their time, but when it comes to cartoons and video games (media still read as something childish), the stigma is much stronger.

**Absolutely true, though if you think about it, despite all this bias, it's not that rare these days to find an article in a mainstream newspaper about Studio Ghibli or Evangelion — animated works with real artistic scope, ones that made it into movie theaters and therefore earned a "mature" treatment: with video games, it seems really hard to see that same kind of treatment, and when there's even a bit of effort, it usually just focuses on the tech side, ignoring all that cultural influence we've been talking about.**

Keep in mind that when we talk about Studio Ghibli, we're talking about something that's been granted an artistic legacy that the rest of the anime landscape really hasn't gotten at all.

People talk about Evangelion, for instance, because it made a boatload of money, not for its artistic value or its Kabbalah references — but generally speaking, these are exceptions.

**I don't know about that — personally, I think Hideaki Anno put the Kabbalah stuff into Evangelion mostly because it "looked cool," but I don't think it would be that strange to find a newspaper article analyzing the work and highlighting themes like depression, escapism, and so on.**

**Think about your own point, actually: the very fact that a Studio Ghibli film gets (rightly) recognized as a work of art is already a huge deal.**

**I don't think there's a video game that's been "accepted" to that same degree by our mainstream media.**

Like I said, I think it's exactly because gaming is seen as an alienating experience, and therefore never entirely positive — at least that's the sense I get from reading around and talking to people.

As for exceptions, I did happen to read a nice article on an online outlet about *Journey*, but mostly because it was treated more like a film than a video game… after all, you can finish it in two or three hours, and, sticking with the theme of your book, you don't spend too much time on it.

**That actually reminds me that when we say "video game," we're talking about *Journey*, *Candy Crush*, a football sim, and *Final Fantasy VII* all at once, and if the category "video game" literally includes everything, we could say that this whole "alienating" quality is kind of the common thread running through it:**

**the time, focus, and commitment a game demands really can't compare to what's needed to watch a movie, or other things that let us "switch our brains off": with a video game that's just not possible, precisely because our actions are the engine that keeps the game moving.**

And that's exactly why it gets treated differently: being active, focused, immersed in these realities that never quite match up with actual reality.

When you're playing you can't really do anything else; watching a movie, you'll never be immersed in that same active way you were describing, and I think that's really the whole difference. Though I could be wrong!

**Same goes for me! On that note, I'd like to bring up a few things I've been researching lately, which you can probably speak to far more authoritatively than I can:**

**back in the '30s in the United States, the Payne Fund Studies were launched — research projects meant to analyze the influence movies were having on children and teenagers.  
  
Movies offered what were effectively perceived as behavioral models, and some studies showed just how much kids' habits and dependencies were shaped by them (who knows how many people started smoking thanks to Humphrey Bogart!)**

**Don't you think video games are something far more powerful from that same angle, and therefore potentially more dangerous too? Thinking about it, maybe they were for us too.**

Well, the moment you have an active role, you naturally create a process of emulation. I think the real danger lies in the fact that age ratings for games don't get respected: simply put, if a game is rated 16+, you shouldn't be buying it for a twelve-year-old, because they're not equipped to properly understand and process it.

I can play a shooter, because I'm able to tell and understand that I'm not doing "something good," that I'm venting my frustrations by shooting Star Wars characters in *Battlefront*.

An eleven-year-old playing a shooter gets influenced by it in a completely different way: maybe they'll start building blowguns and shooting the neighbor's chickens (I've actually seen this happen!).

So it's a question of maturity. People need to be aware, and parents especially need to be aware, that a kid under fourteen misunderstands almost *everything* to some degree.

I mean, Piaget wasn't an idiot, and he always argued that before age fourteen there isn't yet a linear way of thinking: it's after fourteen, after hormonal development kicks in, that a kid's sense of *self* starts taking real shape.

**What you're saying is definitely true, but I'm a little skeptical about the "after": I'm not entirely sure a kid over sixteen is completely immune to these dynamics either. Honestly, I wouldn't bet on it even for adults (myself included): maybe we won't end up shooting at chicken coops, but we could still be affected by something a bit more subtle. I'm thinking mostly of teenagers, but like I said, I think it applies to everyone to some degree, right?**

But when you're older, you're still mediated by abstract thought. Sure, it can still stir up some underlying aggression in you, you might start snapping at people… but a kid doesn't have that mediation at all, precisely because their abstract-thinking brain hasn't developed yet, they're missing those *filters*.

Obviously a fourteen- or fifteen-year-old today isn't exactly sensory-deprived — if anything, it's the opposite, they're overstimulated. Thinking about that, it makes sense that everything should be pushed a bit later, and that families always need to stay involved.

But little kids… little kids just don't have a clue, plain and simple! I see it: between the first and third year of middle school, there's a huge range. I won't even tell you what I see in the last year of elementary school…

**Honestly, comparing notes with other teachers, I've heard some pretty wild stories too.**

The problem obviously starts with families, with parents.

Paradoxically, I think those of us who grew up playing a lot of video games can actually be a lot more on top of things than plenty of other people. After all, certain kinds of games do make your brain work.

**Building on that, how do you picture video games evolving in the near future?**

**Do you think that, with the inevitable generational turnover, the way we talk about them will change too?**

I think *Sword Art Online* saw it coming: augmented and virtual reality will keep expanding. We'll get to some kind of neural-level gaming, or something like that.

**In a much more basic sense, Zuckerberg does seem to be heading that way: the infamous metaverse, which looks like a kind of prototype Matrix.**

Right, and it'll get harder and harder for kids to tell reality and fiction apart, since the two keep blending together more and more.

And it won't be easy for those of us watching from the sidelines either, because our generation doesn't have the tools for it.

I'd like to think everyone I know who spends a lot of time gaming is a pretty "together" person, so maybe the kids and grandkids of us gamers will have a different approach, because we'll have something to pass on to them.

But I also see people my age raising kids right now who don't have anything to pass on, and those kids are basically left to figure it out on their own.

What could end up happening is that current generations go through a lot of negative experiences, and end up being the ones able to actually communicate them to whoever comes after.

Either way, I think we'll see an ever-deeper merging between the interactive side of things and reality, plus more of that *hikikomori*-style drift, with people consciously rejecting the outside world to stay inside the game, or people who genuinely can't tell the two apart anymore.

**I touch on this a bit in the book, and I've realized how complicated it is to discuss, given the polarized environment we live in: on one hand, video games get worshipped like a religion, and on the other, as we've seen, they get dismissed as something harmful and childish.**

Let's say gamers tend to form their own separate group, and don't really get seen as someone who can share their experience with anyone besides other people who game the same way they do. That's the sense I get, partly from watching how kids behave too.

**We're almost at the end of our chat, so before I let you go I want to ask the musician-slash-gamer a few fun questions:**

**in the last few years, "video game music" has become a well-established genre: *chiptune*, *nintendocore*, YouTube covers, orchestras performing Nobuo Uematsu's soundtracks…**

I have a friend who got called to play in the latest *Distant Worlds* tour (I begged for a fifteenth clarinet spot but no luck…)

**I went to see that concert in 2017: absolutely gorgeous.**

The lineup changes every time, depending on where they're performing.

**I bet! But speaking of which, as a musician, what do you think of the phenomenon from a purely musical standpoint?**

**In a way, unlike film or TV soundtracks, video game music has ended up clearly shaping the sound of several music genres: I'm thinking, for instance, of artists who incorporate synthesized sounds inspired by the audio chips of old consoles.**

Honestly, from a strictly compositional standpoint there's nothing new there — those kinds of experiments were already happening back in the '50s and '60s in certain avant-garde music schools.

All of that applies to people composing video game music today too, even in dedicated courses… actually, there are specific courses on video game music now:

there's one at a conservatory that leans heavily on that same avant-garde tradition, as well as minimalist music.

Let's say the music itself is one thing, the sound design is another, you kind of have to split the work into two.

**I'm guessing you mean the whole concept of the *loop*, right?**

**A track that has to repeat basically forever, because as long as the player doesn't move on, the music can't change.**

Exactly, and effects help a lot there in creating that repetitiveness along with the slight variation you need.

The minimalist approach, at the end of the day, is basically that: you have a set of measures you can repeat as many times as you want:

it's like a living organism, changing just a tiny bit every so many measures. Steve Reich's music works more or less like that.

**I think the ambient world too (Brian Eno comes to mind) is built on a similar concept.**

Probably, we're always drawing from that same well: the '50s-60s.

**So basically, even though it's cool to use Game Boy sound chips or try to recreate PlayStation-era string samples and so on, from what you're telling me, nothing particularly innovative is actually happening there.**

From a musical-technical standpoint, yeah. But keep in mind all of twentieth-century music gets recycled: partly in film scores, partly in video game music.

There's really a bit of everything in there, even late Prokofiev, Shostakovich… and then obviously, in what you're describing, there's a whole nostalgia and retro-gaming angle too, with those 16-channel MIDI setups still getting used a lot.

**I'll admit I've personally never really liked the sound of the NES, Game Boy, and so on…**

Well, three-channel audio!

**…but I do really love the soundfonts used on the Super Nintendo, and honestly if I hear the synths from Final Fantasy VI I lose my mind, though maybe that's mostly nostalgia talking.**

Sure, it's still a niche, targeted choice, tied precisely to nostalgia.

What's more interesting is the blending of old sample libraries with new ones: nowadays, for instance, one common base library used has sampled orchestral instruments.

**So basically indistinguishable from a real orchestra: they've automated you all, you're not even needed anymore!**

Exactly! In 70-80% of video game soundtracks, these incredibly advanced libraries get used, running on extremely powerful servers, and that lets you build a score and lay the sounds on top of it down to the millimeter.

Think of it like a Vocaloid, but with every instrument in the orchestra.

**I'm guessing there's obviously a cost angle behind all this: using these libraries costs way less than hiring an actual orchestra, especially for small-to-medium developers.**

Exactly right, but in some cases people still go with a real orchestra: for instance, one of the great things about *Final Fantasy VII Remake* is that almost everything is recorded by an actual orchestra.

**Maybe that matters more in Japan, at least for the biggest productions.**

Yeah, definitely, they also care a lot about vocals, which all get recorded live.

**Last question, and you only get one answer: your favorite game!**

*Final Fantasy X*, a huge love of mine, played millions and millions of times!

**Great choice, I'll resist quoting Tidus's laugh! Thank you so much, Annalisa!**

Thank you, talk soon!
