> *"Which are reality? Which are illusions? One cannot tell until one awakes... Or perhaps they are, at the same time, both truth and fiction... A vast nebulous... With no boundaries... An emptiness equivalent to my own existence... I dreamt such a dream... A long... Never-ending, dream."*
>
> *(Fei, Xenogears)*

The word "delusion" comes from the Latin *in + ludere*, literally "to enter into play."

In 2018, the World Health Organization added gaming disorder to the ICD (*International Classification of Diseases*).

Now: this book obviously doesn't claim to tackle the topic from a clinical angle, but aims to frame it the way it's perceived by the gaming community itself. In my view, we're talking about a problem that's *massively* underestimated, and in some ways that makes sense: in a few decades, those of us over 30-40 will be among the first generations of older adults who grew up on video games from early childhood.

We could say that Gen X and Gen Y[^20] were the *first impact* generations when it comes to the gaming world, especially online gaming (we'll come back to this in the "Old School" chapter).

In practice, certain reflections simply couldn't have been made by anyone before us.

> Underlining just how much gaming spans every age group and occasion, the ESA (2011) found that **a full 72% of Americans play games on a computer or console**.  
>   
> What's more, in the United States, **the average gamer's age is a surprising 37, and 29% of the total gaming population is over 50,** with fairly limited gender differences (42% women, 58% men).[^21]

Given all that, I found myself wondering what my fellow gamers actually thought about *game disorder*.

In many places it's genuinely hard to gauge public opinion on this issue, since there's a lot of confusion around it and the term "gambling addiction" often gets used loosely and incorrectly for something else entirely, so I started my research at the internet's ultimate town square: *Reddit*.

There, I discovered an entire community dedicated to gaming abstinence:

**r/StopGaming/**

![A meme about living the PC gaming dream](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file10.png)

It wasn't much of a surprise — abstinence is pretty trendy on Reddit. You can find entire communities dedicated to:

*fasting* (intermittent fasting)

*nosurf* (abstaining from the internet and social media)

*nofap* (abstaining from masturbation)

and plenty more, so running into a gaming-themed variant was pretty predictable. Still, I'll admit that spending time on *r/StopGaming/* felt like opening Pandora's box. Here's a post I picked at random, published right around the time I'm writing these lines.

> I'm done with RPGs. I'm disgusted and f\*\*\*\*ing tired of grinding, of hours wasted that I could have spent improving myself and society instead. In reality I'm not a dragon-slaying warrior, I'm basically the opposite. I'm 22, unemployed, overweight, I live with my mom, I smell like sweat and Doritos, I have no friends in real life, and if I don't do something now it's only going to get worse.  
> Better late than never, I guess.[^22]

*Note: for the meaning of some of these terms, see the glossary in this book's appendix.*

In a way, reading posts like that selfishly made me feel better:

*Thank god I never got that bad.*

*There are people worse off than me.*

A sigh of relief.

And then I felt like a jerk.

And I also realized how dangerous an alibi *"there's always someone worse off"* really is:

*I only drink one bottle of wine a day, that guy drinks three.*

*Practically a teetotaler by comparison.*

Clearly, this applies to any addiction.

I think this should be something we gamers reflect on first and foremost: as I said, our community is only going to keep growing and consolidating with every passing generation.

If you have any doubts about that, consider this fairly recent data point: during the first week of January 2022, the Steam platform surpassed 28 million users connected to its servers at the same time.[^23]

A figure roughly comparable to half the population of a mid-sized European country, all active at that same instant, on just *one* of the many gaming platforms out there.

Given how widespread this has become, it's worth asking whether the label "gamer" even makes sense anymore.

According to Elle McCarthy (a vice president at Electronic Arts), gaming has become so common and widespread that the term "gamer" no longer really means anything.[^24]

Maybe it's a little early to say that (at least in some corners of the world), but before long the gamer will be as common as the twentieth-century TV viewer — probably even more so: I think that's also why terms like "*casual gamer*" and "*pro gamer*" were coined in the first place.

If everyone plays, then the *tribe* of gamers stops existing as such, because it now includes basically all of humanity, so anyone who wants to stand out from the crowd has to invent new terms.

This dynamic shows up in a lot of group-forming phenomena (television, music, fashion, etc.), but among gamers it's always led to particularly intense forms:

the need to feel part of a clearly defined, and physiologically "hierarchical," community — tied to levels, trophies, experience points, and status. It's no accident that, since the earliest days of online gaming, we've talked casually about associations, guilds, and *clans*.

Getting back to my research, from Reddit I soon landed on GameQuitters, a self-described *"Video Game Addiction Support Community"* founded by content creator Cameron Adair.

In a sense, the same clan logic I just described applies perfectly to this anti-gaming world too:

![The GameQuitters logo](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file11.png)

Adair, drawing on his own personal story, has essentially built a business made up of forums, a TED Talk, YouTube videos, university lectures, respectable partnerships, and above all a paid *"Respawn Program."*

Honestly?

My overall impression of the whole thing wasn't very positive, and after digging through the site, my first thought was, *"I'm too European for this kind of thing."*

I'll admit it: I don't really appreciate the style, tone, or structure of GameQuitters, which I think offers a response to the problem that's just about as unhealthy as the problem itself.

There's a but, though: the very fact that this large, diverse community exists confirms that something really is wrong.

Adair (a fellow millennial) traces his gaming addiction back to when he was ten years old.

In his videos and posts, he describes how gaming disorder led him to drop out of school, and for years had him playing around *16 hours a day* holed up in his parents' basement.

Today he's managed to turn that experience into a livelihood that, even if it raises some eyebrows as I mentioned, does have the merit of shining a spotlight on a problem that's often misunderstood or hidden away:

there are kids out there whose entire existence is essentially *devoted* to gaming.

Setting the psycho-technological angle aside, I think to find the underlying causes we need to look at the economic condition of the Western world:

between widespread job insecurity, anxiety, fierce competitiveness, and the long shadow of the 2007-2013 Great Recession, that latent need to *escape reality* has become more and more common.

At the same time, video games have become relatively cheap, varied, and therefore accessible.  
Today, all it takes is a few euros and an old laptop (or a smartphone) to game — something unthinkable in the '90s or even the early 2000s.

I think the situation is a bit like the relationship between cinema and the 1929 economic crash:

> Faced with an unstable world shaken by economic and political crises, cinema was the most accessible path to escape and rest, found in the great stories Hollywood produced.[^25]

For that matter, another example of modern escapism can be found in the already-mentioned *hikikomori* phenomenon in Japan: born in the late '80s and exploding in the early 2000s (with Japan's early-'90s economic crisis in between), this need to escape is tightly linked to *otaku* culture, made up of video games, manga, and anime.  
And it's precisely in animation that *the need to escape* found its most masterful expression through Hideaki Anno, creator of the masterpiece *Neon Genesis Evangelion* (1995), who in 2021 literally exorcised otaku escapism with the animated film *EVANGELION: 3.0+1.0 THRICE UPON A TIME*.

> As a creator, I want to make things that entertain but that have depth.  
> I didn't want Evangelion to be an escape from reality, I wanted viewers to feel encouraged to live their own lives.[^26]

Unlike film and television, video games carry a considerably more complex structure. Once again, we have to reckon with the time factor, as Jenkins nicely points out in his analysis of the cross-media nature of *The Matrix* franchise.

> Some critics complained that the character of Niobe steals the spotlight from Morpheus in *The Matrix Revolutions*, as if a secondary character were pushing a well-established protagonist out of the picture.  
> What we feel for Niobe, though, will depend on whether or not we played *Enter the Matrix*: if we did, we may have spent hundreds of hours controlling her character, against less than four hours spent watching Morpheus on screen; fighting to keep her character alive and guide her through her mission will have created an intense bond that viewers who only saw her in a handful of scenes simply couldn't feel.[^27]

The more time we spend in a virtual world, the stronger and more intense the emotions we attach to it become: Japanese entertainment saw this coming too, just think of franchises like *.hack* or *Sword Art Online*.

Video games carry a kind of DNA that, even in its more virtuous forms, tries to "trap" the player:

> Even in "healthy" play, the player is "manipulated" by the game: through the mechanism of challenge and the dynamics of desire, the game pushes the player to keep playing, even going so far as to alter their perception of time.  
> But playful practice is born from an act of will. In other words, even if during a play session the player is encouraged to keep playing to prolong the flow experience, they can always decide to stop or interrupt it.  
> In addictive practice, however, the player is no longer driven by an act of will, but plays because they cannot do otherwise: they cannot not play. The relationship between subject and object of the addiction is completely tipped toward the latter: the player is entirely played by the game, becoming a slave to their own object of desire.  
> It's largely because of this loss of freedom, and the shift from the realm of will to the realm of necessity, that we struggle to think of addiction as a "practice" at all: the actor is acted upon more than acting, or rather acts as if possessed by their own object of desire.[^28]

This "manipulation" also ties into the whole *replayability* conversation: as Riolo points out, building on Walton,[^29] a game can play out differently every time, in all sorts of ways.

For example, on a second playthrough, our protagonist in *The Witcher* might make completely different choices compared to our first experience with the game, and note: we're talking about choices both in narrative terms (picking a side) and in pure gameplay terms (gear choices, skills, etc.).  
For that matter, the player is in a sense encouraged by these *What if?* scenarios that invite them to spend even more hours in a game they've already finished: we'll dig into this more in a few pages when we look at the concepts of longevity and replayability in games journalism.

Digging deeper into gaming disorder, I came across a really interesting exchange on the Instagram account of a psychologist who focuses on gaming issues:

> **Question:** "I'm scared to buy my daughter a PlayStation because I don't want her to get addicted."
>
> **The psychologist's answer**: "People very often confuse strong emotional engagement with a video game with being addicted to video games. A video game is an engaging activity, exactly like reading a good book, except it's viewed with more suspicion because it's seen as a harmful pastime. Kids have a much more fluid sense of time than we adults do, which is why they need outside help: it's important to set play times (day and time) together with younger kids."[^30]

Honestly, I have some reservations about how well "we adults" manage our own time, but the substance of what she's saying is really interesting.

The problem is that, in fighting the suspicion mentioned above, we gamers risk underestimating the real damage this way of spending time can cause.

To be clear, most video games aren't to blame, and it's not fair to generalize: that would be like blaming cinema for nearsightedness or music for hearing loss.  
  
Ideally, society should be asking itself what's driven the widespread spread of escapism over the last few decades, increasingly entrenching the need for so many security blankets.

Still, we do have to consider how some developers are playing a very dangerous game of their own, often backed by satellite industries all too ready to churn out controllers, colorful PC cases, and ergonomic gaming chairs, milking gamers every way they can without worrying too much about the consequences.

## 3.1 Developers as Pushers
> *This could be the start of a beautiful business relationship. After all, you're a conniving, backstabbing, two-bit thief... and I'm a convicted psychotic killer and drug dealer*
>
> *(Tommy Vercetti, GTA: Vice City)*

Let's not beat around the bush:

most developers *want* to maximize the hours a gamer dedicates to their creation.

While that seems natural enough, we have to consider that, in certain contexts, developers care about players' health about as much as tobacco companies care about smokers'.

Let's take a look at the most-played video games in the world:

across various platform and console rankings, the names that keep popping up are *Fortnite, League of Legends, Dota 2, Minecraft, Crossfire, Counter-Strike: GO, PUBG, Apex Legends*.

Among them, we can spot a few common features:

- Online games, potentially endless.

- Multiplayer, with entire communities behind them.

- Often free…

- …but with microtransactions

Let's focus for a moment on microtransactions, and specifically on *loot boxes* and similar mechanics.

To simplify the concept, first of all for myself (and pointing you again to the *Gaming Glossary* in this book's appendix), we could say these "loot boxes" generally work like the good old pack of trading cards, meaning:

- You spend a certain amount of money

- You buy a "pack"

- You hope there's something rare inside (often purely cosmetic, meaning an item that doesn't affect gameplay by making you stronger)

And if you don't get what you were after, what do you do?

Simple: *you try again*.

From there, falling into that vicious "*try again, you'll be luckier next time!*" cycle happens in an instant:

this system, which borders on gambling, is often the main source of revenue for a lot of "free" games.

Coming back to the whole time discussion, it's interesting to note how much this factors into certain kinds of microtransactions typical of mobile games:

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file12.png)

> ![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file13.png)

Basically, *the game pushes you to pay in order to save time* that would otherwise be spent on tedious tasks.

It's a bit like the flip side of certain trophies/achievements that instead *encourage* the player to spend time on repetitive actions that are an end in themselves: defeat 1000 enemies, collect every item, build every type of structure, and so on.

But then, where does all that time saved through pay-to-skip microtransactions actually go?

Usually, back into *playing*.

> I just spent 70 euros on gold coins and 240 hours of combat to properly build up this character, there's no way I can just abandon it now!

So we end up in yet another vicious cycle that often turns into genuine gambling addiction, which, again, is a very different issue from gaming disorder.

In other cases still, like in the famous *Pokémon Go*, paying isn't a shortcut to skip boring parts of the game, but a way to *keep playing* without having to wait for daily bonuses.

Daily bonuses themselves are another interesting thing to look at:

some developers encourage players to log in by offering rewards, or by "punishing" absent players, as in games like *Farmville* or *Candy Crush*.

If we ignore the game for too long, our virtual farm's harvest is lost forever… unless, of course, we use a small microtransaction to fix that.

## 3.2 China's Response

> *That's right, fall to your knees... just like every other man who dared challenge me!*
>
> *(Chun Li, Ultra Street Fighter II: The Final Challengers)*

Unlike what happens in our own free-for-all Wild West, in China the video game issue is treated as a very serious matter that the government has recently taken to addressing quite often, turning its Orwellian eye toward younger players and online gaming:

> One hour a day, up to three times a week. And only on weekends: 8 to 9 PM, Friday through Sunday. Plus a bonus during national holidays. That's it.
>
> Xi Jinping's China has rolled out a crackdown on online gaming for minors, meaning anyone under 18. Less than 48 hours after the start of the new school year, the National Press and Publication Administration issued a notice "for the effective prevention of minors' addiction to online games," aiming, according to state media, to tackle the long-standing problem of gaming addiction\*. This way, "the physical and mental health of minors will be more effectively protected."[^31]

*\* My note: the term used by the reporting outlet is technically the one used for gambling addiction, a genuinely different issue from gaming disorder.*

Leaving aside ethical questions and personal-freedom concerns (which would take us too far from this book's subject), an obvious question comes up:

*Why is China so hung up on video games?*

The World Economic Forum tried to offer some answers:

> The new gaming controls are just one piece of a much broader set of government policies aimed at young people's behavior.
>
> Other aspects include cracking down on "effeminate" male imagery in pop culture, and on entertainment sources with "incorrect political positions."[^32]

Basically, we're talking about social control, with restrictions similar to those seen around internet use in general, and social media in particular.

As already noted, this isn't the place to unpack the freedom-limiting substance of these restrictions, but I think it's yet another sign of just how crucial a role video games play in modern society.

Another striking figure from the World Economic Forum is, once again, tied to time: on average, people in China play 12.39 hours a week, well above the global average.

![](/img/libri/too-much-gaming/file14.png)

Data from Limelight.com

Cynically speaking, young Chinese players could be spending those 12.39 hours studying aerospace engineering instead:

or at least, that's probably the kind of reasoning that went through Xi Jinping's head and that of his colleagues, who were willing to make some major Chinese companies with skin in the game, like Tencent or NetEase, cry over their stock price.

According to the *South China Morning Post*, an outlet tied to the Xinhua News Agency once called video games "spiritual opium," only to walk it back after Tencent's shares took too much of a hit.[^33]

In short: we're way beyond the doom-and-gloom warnings about kids' brains you'd get from your average worried Western politician, and, even if in a negative light, the medium gets looked at through a completely different lens over there.

China has historically had a complicated relationship with gaming, and provided one striking example of it between 2000 and 2014:

during those fourteen years, the world's most important consoles (PlayStation, Xbox, and the entire Nintendo lineup) were banned, and, in response, the PC market saw extraordinary growth:

> There are 312 million PC gamers in China. By 2023, analysts predict that number will reach 354 million, surpassing the entire population of the United States and generating an estimated \$16 billion in revenue.[^34]

Arcades and mobile gaming rode the same wave, finding extraordinarily fertile ground in the People's Republic — perfect conditions for developers and game studios to grow too.

Through all this, Chinese teams today sit firmly at the top of the *esports* rankings, which in the country aren't just played, but followed even more closely than conventional sports.[^35]

Obviously, when we talk about China we're talking about a huge range of realities that can be extremely different from one another, but bottom line, one thing is clear:

for better or worse, the Chinese take gaming very seriously.

*Note: shortly after finishing this chapter (November 2021), China shut down Fortnite's local servers.*

## 3.3 The Fortnite Phenomenon

> *I thought you'd be bigger.*
>
> *(Bunker Jonesy, Fortnite)*

Talking with kids and teenagers, and asking around among their parents, I realized just how much of a stronger common thread *Fortnite* is than I expected.

Obviously I already knew how incredibly popular *Fortnite* was, but I hadn't quite grasped how, in some ways, it transcends the very concept of a video game:

in certain circles, *"I don't play Fortnite"* isn't just the modern equivalent of *"I don't have a PlayStation"* back in 1997 — it sounds more like *"I don't have a PlayStation, a Nintendo 64, or a PC, I don't watch TV, and I hate soccer too."*

And yes, in theory we're talking about a *single* video game, and yet for a lot of preteens, not playing *Fortnite* means being completely cut off from the world, as if it somehow absorbed the importance of several different media all at once (in this sense, I think of it as the gaming equivalent of Instagram or TikTok).

As we've seen, the fact that *Fortnite* is "free" but loaded with microtransactions makes it incredibly accessible on one hand, while at the same time plugging it into all those unhealthy dynamics that can affect both your health and your wallet.

On top of that, its sheer reach has generated some genuinely peculiar phenomena: there's a form of cyberbullying, for instance, aimed at players who use "default skins," meaning the basic (and therefore free) look for their character.

The word "default," which echoes the well-worn term *noob*,[^36] has become a put-down used by kids both online and in real life.[^37]

Compared to the *noob* label mentioned above, which was strictly tied to a player's skill and experience, with *default* we find a social bias more comparable to fashion trends in clothing:

the *skin*, and therefore the character's look, doesn't affect gameplay or give any material advantage, and yet it becomes a central way for a player to stand out within the social context of the game-verse.

A confession: I don't have a ton of hands-on experience with *Fortnite*, and if I dug deeper into it, it was purely to write this book:

I think it would be impossible to write about video games without making this one a case study, given that, as I write these lines (late 2021), *Fortnite* is the *most-played video game in history*, with more than 90 billion hours collectively spent on it.[^38]

The debate around the game has drawn in not just advocacy groups, but governments and the unlikeliest public figures:

a couple of years ago, for example, a member of the British royal family went as far as calling for the game to be banned outright, citing its addictive effects.[^39]

So we're a long way from the controversy that surrounded *Grand Theft Auto III* back in 2001 (which, in some ways, may have even contributed to its success) — we're talking about a completely new phenomenon, without the openly "criminal" veneer of Rockstar Games' series.

The core of the controversy around *Fortnite* isn't really about violence, but is baked right into its merits — namely, just how much players love spending their free time on it:

according to *Business Insider*, *Fortnite*'s success comes above all from its adaptability, the way the game has essentially turned itself into a kind of ongoing *service*.[^40]

Constant updates, being cross-platform, and matches that wrap up in a manageable amount of time (around 20 minutes) are the foundation of this giant's staggering success.

In a ludic sense, *Fortnite* could be considered an emblem of cultural convergence.

A few examples can help illustrate its media reach:

Disney, Jordan, and the extremely wealthy NFL have all invested in *Fortnite* for promotional purposes, spending amounts that would make any TV network's day.

After all, advertising goes wherever it can meet the target audience for whatever it's selling, and a *Fortnite* in-game event probably captures the attention of most of the world's teenagers far more effectively than any tired old TV commercial ever could.

Given all this, as I said, you have to wonder whether it's still fair to call *Fortnite* "just" a video game:

maybe it would be more accurate to think of it as one of the first real examples of a *metaverse*.

## 3.4 Time Distortions

> *History abhors paradoxes*
>
> *(Raziel, Soul Reaver II)*

While studying the crucial relationship between video games and time, I came across the excellent *Time Out: How Video Games Distort Time*, a study written by media scholar Agata Meneghelli in 2013, which I've already had a chance to cite.

It's a remarkably interesting read, and what struck me most was its analysis of that mental "short circuit" that makes us lose track of time while gaming:

> play, like any practice, requires time and unfolds within time, but also because game-time is characterized as separate, autonomous from the time of ordinary life.  
>   
> This separation doesn't just come from the fact that a game has a beginning and an end, but more generally from the fact that time within the game flows differently from time outside the game: it's a time with its own internal structure, following its own rules and logic, which may be more or less close to the rules that shape the time of ordinary life.

The book analyzes, using a semiotic approach, the relationship between time and video games, highlighting several fascinating traits that explain the gamer's "pattern," or rather, their tendency to carry out repetitive actions (often even frustrating or boring!) in order to reach a certain goal or level.

These repetitive patterns, once internalized, become the very core of the player's enjoyment:

> In video games, repetition plays a decisive role in integrating action and awareness. As Torben Grodal (2003) points out, repetition is a central element of gaming pleasure, to the point that we can talk about a genuine aesthetics of repetition, characterized by three phases:  
>   
> 1) on first encountering a video game, the player feels a sense of unfamiliarity that pushes them to explore it;  
>   
> 2) after some effort and repetition, the player reaches a point where they can master the game and complete its tasks (mastery);  
>   
> 3) by repeating the acquired patterns of perception, action, and cognition, the player eventually performs them automatically (automation).  
>   
> At first glance, reaching this level of automatism works against a state of flow, since it risks generating a feeling of boredom in the player. But if these automatisms are embedded within narrative rhythms that pair repetition with novelty and difference, they end up becoming functional to achieving an enjoyable experience.

This "aesthetics of repetition" translates into a distortion of time: when we forget about the real world, we naturally tend to perceive the passing of hours differently.

This is especially interesting because, as already noted, no other hobby demands a comparable price in time to that of video games.

> the player is called upon to live both the time of the game and the time of the act of playing — and since these two temporal levels are irreducible to one another, despite being concurrent, the player perceives time in an unstable, bipolar way, constantly oscillating between the two levels.

Beyond time as such, we also need to factor in *concentration* and *engagement*.

You can get distracted watching a movie; with a video game, generally, you can't: in this sense, video games could generally be considered a unique case within the cultural industry as Adorno and Horkheimer conceived of it back in 1947:

> The products of the culture industry **can be relied upon to be consumed even in a state of distraction**. But each one of them is a model of the vast economic mechanism that keeps everyone under pressure from the outset, both in work and in the leisure that resembles it.[^41]

While games certainly do distract us from the real world, the player's attention, concentration, and above all *interaction*, are the fuel that keeps the game's engine running, and without them, it has no way to move forward at all.

This becomes seriously problematic when the gamer becomes addicted, essentially getting vampirized by the very system that allows the game to "exist."

You could argue that sports (another activity requiring repetition, dedication, and concentration) can also make you lose track of time, but generally that's offset by physical and mental wellbeing: a healthy amount of physical activity, after all, is something doctors regularly recommend and encourage.

Gaming, while often good for developing reflexes and knowledge, tends on average to wreck your eyesight and posture, encourages a sedentary lifestyle, and forces you to sit in front of a screen for many hours at a stretch.

To back up some of these unpleasant side effects, it's worth citing Jesus Pujol's study, published in *Annals of Neurology* in 2016:

analyzing a sample of 2,442 children between the ages of 7 and 11, Pujol found that occasional gamers (1 hour of gaming a week or less) showed better motor skills and school performance, while more hardcore players showed behavioral problems, poor social skills, and a tendency toward isolation.[^42]

As we've already seen, in the worst cases gaming can generate a genuine behavioral addiction, and on that very subject, Meneghelli points out another interesting aspect:

> The dimension of time is essential to explaining the shift from playful practice to addictive practice, not only because addicted players tend to play for many consecutive hours and with very high frequency, but above all because, in cases of addiction, game-time tends to devour time outside the game.
>
> For the addicted player, the hierarchy between the time of daily life and the time of the game flips in favor of the latter, which becomes the dominant temporal dimension.

In short, our perception of time shifts quite a bit while we're gaming.

In a sense, we tend to "adjust" our own life's time to match the time built into the game.

## 3.5 The Streamer and the Pro Gamer

> *I am thou, thou art I...*
>
> *(Shin Megami Tensei: Persona series)*

I'll admit I had some trouble understanding the explosion of *gaming voyeurism* that's everywhere these days and that's constituted a kind of industry within the industry for years now: game streaming is a genuinely innovative form of entertainment that boasts millions of viewers and, as such, moves a serious amount of money.

As revealed by the huge Twitch data leak of October 2021,[^43] the earnings of plenty of individual *streamers* could bring more than a few TV networks to tears.

Without dwelling on individual figures, the interesting part is how, with *game streaming*, gaming has become an actual job:

obviously, sometimes the game itself is just an excuse to show off the streamer's flamboyant personality (Twitch content creators quite often focus on completely different things, like commentary, tutorials, music, drawing, etc.), and yet the core of a lot of their success stays true to the platform's origins:

gaming with a solid tech setup behind it.

And here's the obvious question:

why is watching other people play so appealing?

I think there'd be enough material there for a whole other book, but let's linger on it for a moment, since we find an element we've touched on repeatedly in these pages: good old *escapism*:

> Viewers try to use the platform for escapism, to get away from their own daily lives.
>
> \[…\]
>
> It seems likely, then, that we're not just wired to enjoy watching other people play, but also psychologically driven to see streaming platforms as a way to satisfy our informational, social, and escapist needs. This phenomenon creates a demand that Twitch is more than ready to meet.[^44]

So, all roads lead back to *The Matrix*: even though the game-time relationship is front and center here too, it's still obvious that "playing" and "watching someone else play" are two completely different things, with the second one being, in a sense, akin to the passivity of the classic TV viewer.

There's another crucial angle to consider, though: *emulation*. One of the "new dreams" for teenagers today is becoming a professional gamer, following in the footsteps of the many streamers and YouTubers who've managed to turn their passion into a profession.

It sounds a bit contradictory:

it's hard to think of gaming, "leisure" by definition, as a job: in a way, it's a paradox similar to the one surrounding professional athletes.

And in fact, well before the rise of streaming, the first examples of professional gaming can be traced back to esports, which themselves went through a series of ongoing changes:

if back in 2003 a national esports gold medal in *Unreal Tournament* only timidly made the news,[^45] the kind of modern corporate marketing push behind pro gaming today[^46] would, until just a few years ago, have only been conceivable in a cyberpunk sci-fi story.

Let's look at a couple of excerpts (bold mine):

> With in-game matchmaking and third-party premium matchmaking services like ESEA, it's easy to find someone to play with. But if you want to win, **you need to practice**
>
> \[...\]
>
> Professional gamers **practice mechanics tirelessly** to stay competitive and improve little by little, but **players who are just starting out need to put in a lot of practice** to improve quickly.
>
> \[...\]
>
> Still, there's a lot to learn by watching the best players. **Videos and live streams are great resources for learning gameplay techniques** and are a good reference for understanding how to play. Then, as you improve, reviewing your own matches will let you thoroughly analyze your own playstyle and learn from your mistakes. **All the best players do this**.

Just between us?

Those words worry me a little.

To be fair, plenty of people within the industry itself do respond to critical voices, addressing them directly, as with one gaming-performance advocacy site.[^47]

Even so, the tone we find there is fairly questionable:

> Some warnings claim that playing more than 20 hours a week is problematic.
>
> Well, that's like saying an NBA player who trains more than twenty hours a week is addicted to basketball.
>
> Sometimes, or a lot of the time, outsiders just don't understand.

I'm no doctor, but I have serious doubts that twenty hours a week of basketball carries the same risks as twenty hours a week of gaming: it's worth remembering the Jesus Pujol research already cited.

Beyond that, I think it's fair to point out how uncommon it is to run into amateurs able to seriously commit to a sport for what works out to roughly three hours a day, seven days a week.

On the flip side, it's not at all rare to run into gamers who dedicate many hours a day to their hobby, often risking falling into that same cycle of dependency we've already discussed: basketball wasn't designed with those "trapping" mechanics we talked about a few pages back.

That said, it's also worth noting that esports tend to have a fairly unimaginative DNA, and are therefore pretty far removed from the distortions tied to *game disorder* we've already covered.

In a sense, once you go pro, the whole "too much gaming" conversation stops applying, precisely because gaming replaces work and takes up that same space. I think it's important to stress this, because I have no interest in demonizing this world, just in pushing back on some of its communication habits.

In particular, I find that *"outsiders just don't understand"* line pretty unsettling.

It's an alibi anyone can use to justify toxic behavior, maybe legitimizing it with a vague sense of camaraderie:

*"outsiders just don't understand"* is a message that makes you feel like you're not alone.

And it's easy to see the appeal, especially when you actually are.
