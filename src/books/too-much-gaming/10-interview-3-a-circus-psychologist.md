> *Manuela Vinci is a psychologist, gamer, and cosplayer, and part of a small circus arts collective.*

Interview conducted on 2/17/2022

**Hi Manuela! Let's start with a quick introduction: can you tell me what you do, and what you studied?**

Hi! So, I'm a psychologist and I'm specializing in systemic-relational psychotherapy, which means I work with all kinds of clients: adults, kids, couples, families… it's no accident that systemic-relational psychology is also called systemic-family psychology.

I'd say I do a bit of everything, but my goal is to become an oncology psychologist, so I'm working toward training in oncology and hospital-based psychology. That decision became even clearer after the pandemic: if I was 98% sure before, after COVID I'm at 100%.

**As a "potential patient" myself, I think about that a lot too: after the pandemic, we all ended up with pretty frayed nerves, and I think for a lot of people going to therapy could be a huge help.**

**The trouble is it's brutally hard to deal with the endless wait times in the public healthcare system and the staff shortages, or at least I imagine so.**

You're absolutely right, and actually there's a lot of talk right now about having a "primary-care psychologist" available to everyone (I'm actually writing an article about exactly that).

Unfortunately, at the national level there's a ton of resistance, though thankfully that sometimes eases up at the local level: where I live, for instance, a proposal for exactly this kind of primary-care psychologist program was approved unanimously — the idea is that within a year or two a lot of psychologists will be trained and placed into community health centers, extending that kind of support across the healthcare system more broadly.

Right now, if someone doesn't have the means to pay for therapy privately, I'll be honest, I'm not sure I'd recommend they go through the public system:

anything helps, don't get me wrong, but like you said, staff are overloaded and wait times are huge, so the public system generally can't keep a patient in care for as long as they'd actually need, precisely because it has to follow preset bureaucratic timelines.

**And obviously we're not talking about problems that get solved with an aspirin or a handful of sessions.**

**I think it's even more complicated because therapy still doesn't seem to be all that well "digested" by society here, unlike, say, in English-speaking countries. And on top of that, the people who'd need support the most are exactly the ones with the fewest resources, who are often naturally reluctant to even consider talking to a psychologist.**

True: you mentioned English-speaking countries, and in fact, in England, for example, where psychological support is well established, a study showed that in places where people are happier, that actually translates into a better overall economy, precisely because when people are happy, everything runs better.

Which just goes to show that even the mythical "psychologist bonus" some governments talk about, which might look like a big expense for the state, should really be seen as an investment with a solid return.

**Then again, given the constant cuts to education and healthcare, I guess we shouldn't be too surprised to find resistance here too.**

Yeah, it's pretty shameful.

**On a happier note, let's get straight to the book's topic: what was your first video game, or your first encounter with this whole world?**

Well, my memories are a bit fuzzy: I come from a family where video games were always a big presence, starting with my dad… so I got to know video games when I was really little, and even when I wasn't playing myself, I'd watch someone else play.

I think my first memory is of a *Final Fantasy* game…

**In a sense, you're kind of a pioneer of game streaming, then. So your dad's a gamer!**

Absolutely! And still is, actually: him, my brother… actually, to be honest, I'm the one in my family who games the least.

I'd say my "debut" was with *Final Fantasy* as a spectator (I think it was *Final Fantasy VII*, I vividly remember my first encounter with Aerith!), while as an actual player, I'd say the various *Super Mario* and *Zelda* games on Nintendo 64, with *Resident Evil* coming a bit later.

**That's really interesting, because while researching for the book, I noticed how much gaming really exploded across Gen X and Gen Y, and at this point I'd say your dad was a genuine pioneer!**

Well, my dad was born in '64, so yeah!

I even remember, before my parents split up, arguments where my mom would say *"I don't play anymore, but I was the one who played the first platformers!"* and I'd be sitting there wondering what kind of family I'd ended up in…

**Well, that's a pretty lucky family history! You can proudly claim your gaming lineage. But sticking with the present, tell me:**

**if you had to estimate how many hours a week you play now, what would you say?**

*(laughs)* Zero! Who has time to play? Maybe some phone games to kill time, but that's different.

**Funny, because the other friend I interviewed said almost exactly the same thing! She's got a great gaming history too, but with how little time she has these days, it's hard for her to commit to anything more involved than the usual solitaire, minesweeper, that kind of thing.**

Makes sense! And keep in mind I've never been a fan of match-based games either, like *League of Legends*, where a session takes 45 minutes.

RPGs aside, I played a ton of *Halo*, but if you tell me I only have half an hour and then it's game over, I'll just skip it entirely, I won't even bother starting. What am I going to do in half an hour? I won't even make it through the menus!

**Yeah, I get it, you'd want to actually settle in for at least a couple of hours.**

Exactly! And at that point I'd rather watch something on Netflix, watch one episode that's under an hour, and be done with it. And it's a shame, honestly…

**I think that, after getting used to it during childhood and adolescence, adjusting to the tight schedule of adult life is a real struggle for every gamer… keep in mind the whole point of this little book is really the relationship between time and video games, specifically how much time gamers end up sacrificing for them:**

**while researching this, I inevitably ended up digging into Gaming Disorder, a condition the World Health Organization added to the ICD (*International Classification of Diseases*) in 2018.**

**Along the way, I also discovered that online communities preaching total gaming abstinence have popped up.**

**Needless to say, I'm really curious to hear your professional opinion on the subject!**

Well: the fact that it got added to the ICD makes sense.

Even though video games serve a purpose and were created for a reason, the very fact that abuse of them is even a topic of conversation inevitably points to some kind of underlying disorder… a disorder that's actually made it into the DSM too, the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, which just goes to show the problem is real.

That said, it's also true that communities preaching total gaming abstinence are just as wrong: in general, extremes are NEVER good, ever.

**Couldn't agree more.**

There are tons of studies showing how mindful use of video games brings real benefits, but obviously it's like anything else… take carbs, for instance:

if you eat a plate of pasta every now and then, that's totally fine, but if you eat it every single day, seven days a week, all year round, you're inevitably going to face some consequences.

The point is that video games do have positive effects: improving coordination, reflexes… if you think about every game where we got stuck and had to figure something out to level up or move forward, you realize those are all problem-solving situations, the same kind (proportionally speaking) that get taught in school through math and logic.

Take memory, for instance: I remember in *Zelda* having to play melodies on the ocarina to make it rain, to attract chickens, and so on.

All of that trains a kind of multitasking that we now find pretty much everywhere. And think, too, about the coordination required in fighting games!

So basically, they're all exercises that, with the right precautions, stimulate the brain.

After all, if they didn't stimulate it, we wouldn't even see the negative pathological consequences, or the abuse that comes with it.

**Right: it's not something we can really compare to film, television, or other more *passive* media, since with video games you're in situations where your brain has to be almost constantly engaged.**

**So more useful and beneficial on one hand, but sneakier on the addiction side.**

**Just to give you an example, the idea for this book came to me during lockdown, when I realized I was playing about three hours a day of *Dark Souls III*: naturally, when you're thirty instead of fifteen, your sense of those hours is completely different.**

Exactly, and that's really tied to time, and it's very relative, like you said, precisely because a three-hour gaming session at fifteen is one thing, and at thirty it's another, and it's natural that it starts raising some real questions for you.

It's actually pretty interesting, too — I read a lot of interviews with kids (sometimes ones who did end up developing an actual disorder) who learned English thanks to video games.

Obviously, that's because, in a multiplayer environment, to interact with whichever Japanese or American player you're teamed up with, you're forced to get comfortable with the universal language you can communicate in, so you end up really motivated to learn English.

Of course, with really long sessions the whole thing risks tipping into something else, but if someone manages to "stop there," video games could genuinely be an incredible tool for learning, and even for improving interpersonal relationships:

I remember when I played *Pokémon Stadium* and would go ask a friend what team they'd put together, it was basically a way of making friends with people.

People used to trade trading cards, now they trade Pokémon: where's the problem, where's the difference?

The difference shows up when what should be a pastime becomes your goal for the entire day, your whole world. That's the point where you can't get back out anymore.

**Exactly, and that's actually the part I find most interesting. You're absolutely right about the dynamics you're describing, and I'd say they were pretty foundational for our generation:**

**in the late '90s, everybody played, so you might end up bringing your Game Boy along, or carrying your controller over to a friend's house to play on their PlayStation.**

**Still, back then, and even more so today, we have to reckon with the other side of the coin you mentioned, the one tied to escapism and the need to step outside reality and forget the real world: think of the *hikikomori* phenomenon in Japan, as well as the possible relational struggles that came out of the pandemic and lockdowns.**

Absolutely. Though, even if the two phenomena can look similar on the surface, they actually have two completely different roots if you dig into their origins.

Obviously the hikikomori phenomenon originated in Japan, where the numbers are genuinely alarming: we're talking about at least 500,000 confirmed cases, but if you count estimated cases too, it goes up to a million.

In my own country, estimated cases are in the tens of thousands, and relative to the size of the population, that's a genuinely concerning number too.

What we experienced during the pandemic is a different matter — there was a real *need* to escape, because being cooped up at home with your family, especially for teenagers who had no other outlet (even the much-hated experience of school itself), became a genuine necessity.

For adults it's more complicated, it would need its own study, but we're definitely talking about a different mechanism, even though the need to escape reality is definitely present here too.

Keep in mind that hikikomori onset happens between ages 10 and 40, but the highest incidence is between 15 and 19, exactly where that escapist need you mentioned matters most. The underlying idea is finding something in the virtual world that the real world isn't giving you.

People assume someone with social phobia shuts themselves away because they want nothing to do with the world, but that's a huge misconception. The reality is these are people who *want* to live in this world, but don't know how. And the only tools and people that let them do that are online, because online offers a different way of perceiving their needs without being bombarded with judgment — a conversation, in other words, that isn't reduced to *"so what did you get on your math test?"*

**Crystal clear, and I firmly believe that "fear of judgment" is closely tied to what you're describing. There's also the fact that, thanks to the internet, we now have the ability to actually choose: before, you had to "make do" and find friends who happened to be nearby, starting with the usual classmates… thinking about it, it's kind of the same point I make about video games:**

**before, you had to make do with whatever you had on hand, in a sense "settle," but that's exactly how you ended up really diving deep into things.**

**In today's world, through the internet, you can make your own personal selection, and if you don't fit in with the people around you, you can find understanding by chatting with someone on the other side of the world playing *Halo* with you, someone you might feel you have more in common with than your own classmates.**

Absolutely, and that's actually a double-edged sword. I'm deeply convinced that pathologies don't necessarily arise out of something bad: they arise out of something good that ends up turning into something bad.

Being able to choose your own games and friends can be bad on one hand, because it does encourage detaching from the reality around you… but if we look at the other side of the coin, it's also a good thing: it's not really all that different from a study-abroad program, where someone decides to go to a certain country and meet new people. Without the resources to actually do that, this need gets channeled online instead.

To this day I have several online friends who let me get to know other regional cultures without ever leaving my hometown: I met a very dear friend online from another city when I was fifteen, and interacting with someone my own age from somewhere else was a huge cultural exchange for me.

**Especially because a couple of decades ago, the differences between different cities and regions were felt much more sharply than today — back when the world was still "analog," certain distances really made themselves felt.**

Exactly! So basically, on one hand it's a positive phenomenon, but it can genuinely get worse through all those escapism mechanisms you were talking about. We've got the good and the bad, like with everything.

**Speaking of escapism, I'd like to run a historical thought by you: in the book I draw a parallel between the 1929 crash and the rise of the film industry, and what's happening today between young people and video games.**

**What I see in common is this tendency to escape a reality that's more and more full of "anxiety" and "distress," and, at the same time, a drop in the cost/requirements needed to access certain kinds of entertainment:**

**today, all it takes is an old laptop and about ten euros to buy a game on Steam or the Epic Store, something that would've been unthinkable fifteen years ago… meanwhile, society seems to keep sinking into ever-worse crises, with the pandemic and situations like the hikikomori numbers you mentioned earlier.**

**And let's not forget that the kids growing up this way will, at the same time, be watching us become the first generations of "elderly" gamers. How do you see it?**

That thought touches on a lot of themes, and yes, we can say there's a certain parallel between the '29 crash and today's situation if we take sociability as the common thread:

humans are social animals, none of us are anyone in isolation.

Sure, there's also the whole idea that to be well with others, we first need to be well on our own, but the social component is *extremely* important.

The '29 crash, like you said, is a good example because you had people losing their jobs: some managed to grit their teeth and get through it, some ended up taking their own lives, and the kids growing up in that apocalyptic setting — how could they lift their spirits?

By going to the movies, finding a reason to watch a film together… so dreaming, but doing it together, because cinema is also a huge social gathering phenomenon.

With the pandemic, we saw kids with that same need for social connection, mixed with uncertainties and problems comparable to those of '29, except for two years they had no way and no space to actually socialize: in that context, the virtual world became the biggest gathering place on earth, something both positive and negative at the same time.

It always comes back to that: we all had Zoom, House Party, and other tools that helped us get through the 2020 crisis… but you always have to remember that if all of us went through it badly, teenagers went through it even worse.

For them, those two or three hours on House Party turned into nine, because kids have, and had, a much stronger need for social connection than we do.

Coming back to video games in the strict sense, we're definitely the trailblazers here, and we have to consider that we're living through what's maybe the worst period for sociability: we'll probably re-establish some kind of balance eventually, only to fall back into the same problems at some unspecified point in the future.

On the bright side, a lot of organizations have popped up dealing with social isolation and social withdrawal… in that sense, society is taking decent care of kids, the problem is that today's teenagers are carrying the weight of our own baggage, and actually the baggage of the generation before ours too, which is, not coincidentally, where the whole addiction conversation originally started.

**The heavy legacy of Gen X, basically.**

**Coming back to the future, in this scenario, what do you expect from tomorrow's video games?**

**In recent years, we've seen the medium evolve enormously — gaming has, in a sense, contaminated other media and been contaminated right back, everything's become cross-media:**

**we've had the gamification of education, of social media, of music, and so on. At the same time, a lot of games have started basing their revenue on the "loot box" lottery, with developers trying to "trap" players inside their creations and metaverses.**

I find it pretty hard to predict what video games will look like in the future, but here's a thought:

video games have always mirrored society's needs, so we'd need to understand which direction society itself is heading in.

Let's be honest: if video games didn't offer an alternative to reality, and therefore an outlet for needs that, like you said, society itself *doesn't* meet, nobody would play them.

Something like *League of Legends*, technology aside, would have been almost inconceivable thirty years ago, because a lot of ideas have changed since: think, for instance, of the idea of playing a character with a sexual orientation that doesn't necessarily match your own — those are needs born out of today's reality, from 2000 to 2020.

Over the next twenty years, video games will probably end up including a huge range of possibilities that society itself will keep denying elsewhere.

On the technical side, we've had shooters, we've had *Animal Crossing*… it's hard to know what else game studios will come up with in the future, and it makes sense that, in the meantime, that whole "instant reward" mechanic you mentioned carries a lot of weight, since that's exactly what leads to genuine gambling addiction.

I think we'll see a lot more learning-based games: even now, interactive whiteboards get used a lot, which take into account that kids are naturally wired for a certain kind of interactivity — an extremely reactive brain that's going to matter a lot for future video games too.

And the big classic themes will always stick around too, the ones dealing with war, death… but personally, if I had to think about the games I'd spend hours and hours on, I'd talk about ones that span a lot of different, even radically different things: in cinematic terms, think of something like the Marvel universe and how it folds in so many genres with such different tones.

**In a sense, you've partly described the future of *battle royale* games… if we look at *Fortnite*, we could genuinely call it a "virtual universe" more than a video game in the conventional sense.**

**That's interesting for society as a whole, just think about it from an economic angle, where brands like Disney tend to promote themselves on *Fortnite* exactly the way they would on TV or in magazines — which, if anything, get relegated to a more secondary role now, precisely because they no longer carry the same weight with the target audience:**

**these days, a teenage girl is more likely to buy a Fortnite skin than to go looking for a print teen magazine at the newsstand.**

Absolutely, I think that's inevitable, and it's an angle I see as pretty negative for the future.

Not so much the development of video games themselves, which, in expanding their own horizons, still comes back to that same problem-solving mechanic that takes us all the way back to Zelda's chickens (and therefore a form of discovery and learning):

the trouble is, if these universes become the only stage where kids learn about themselves, where they find a sense of belonging, then we're facing a genuine tragedy… if we had a magic wand to rebalance the virtual and the real, I think we'd end up with something that adds real value — a source of dialogue, exchange, experimentation.

**I think that's really the challenge of the future:**

**just to give you an example, while talking with some kids, I found out that in *Fortnite* there's a kind of bullying aimed at people using the base skins (the free ones), a dynamic that mirrors exactly what happens with designer clothes in the real world:**

**or, similarly, being good or bad at a video game has, in certain circles, become comparable to excelling or not at a sport.**

**One way or another, it seems like future generations won't be able to avoid video games on a social level anymore.**

Yeah, that's kind of the consequence of what I was saying before, the two worlds are completely out of balance: I remember when I was a kid, if you didn't have the right brand of jeans or the right designer shirt, you were nobody, you were basically seen as poor… now, with all the affordable budget brands out there, there's been a kind of low-cost revolution, so maybe that whole dynamic has shifted to other areas, and wherever the real and the virtual are out of balance in favor of the virtual, that's where you get the cyberbullying incidents you were referring to.

It's genuinely worrying, because it means kids end up building their sense of self around a virtual performance.

In fact, sometimes being athletic and good at sports can even be seen as a *flaw*, because the world of sports has become so extremely competitive… now, I'm part of the circus world, so my own reality is pretty much the polar opposite of that, and in fact I've seen a lot of kids come into the circus precisely because it's not a competitive environment.

It's as if being on a sports team automatically has to mean reaching a level of performance where you *win*, where you come in first, and there's no longer room for the flip side of *"I'm the nerd, I'm on the computer, you're the athlete, so we both excel, just in two different worlds"* — no!

You have to excel at *whatever you do*.

And in certain types of games, like the ones you mentioned, where you have to buy something "to prove who you are"… that's where we've got a real problem.

A problem you obviously run into a lot less in a single-player game, for instance:

sure, you can platinum it 600 times and feel like a badass, but everyone can still finish it the same way regardless, so you're on a completely different competitive plane.

**Right, and there's a whole angle tied to online games that genuinely worries me if we connect it to recent news about the metaverse:**

**in a way, I think it's actually more worrying than "classic" bullying, precisely because it projects a physical need onto something virtual and intangible that, in practice, stays firmly in the hands of individual private companies — the same companies that can "kill" your character or ban your account entirely at their own discretion:**

**you don't actually own anything, so your image as a winner, as a champion, or whatever it is, is never really yours — it belongs to whichever company made the game.**

That mechanism is tragic, and it's exactly what triggers cyberbullying, because if you actually owned something, you'd be a bully in a different way:

the very fact that character *isn't yours*, that you don't own anything, somehow means you have to dominate the other person in a different way — by insulting them, by crushing them as an opponent, enemy, or even teammate.

You used exactly the right word: intangible. If I don't own anything, how do I assert myself over someone else?

I inevitably have to destroy "something" belonging to the other person, so that I can prove my own existence.

It's a mechanism that's simplistic, senseless, and dangerous, one game companies probably underestimate, maybe because it's really a whole chain of causes rooted in our society, which is what ends up letting all that rot show through.

**And in fact, we see this same aggression, this same drive to crush other people, constantly on social media too, or in the workplace — we've basically got a twisted, warped version of capitalism applied to technological development.**

**Stepping outside that topic, there's another question I'd like to ask you: like I asked the other friend I interviewed, I'd love your "female" perspective on the gaming world.**

**My own perception, as a guy, is that compared to twenty years ago, gaming today is a lot more accepted among women, and yet in the mainstream it still seems to be experienced, and especially talked about, as a pretty much exclusively male universe, and a lot of gaming communities really do seem pretty closed-off and sexist to me. Do you think there's been a real change, or is that just my impression?**

No, it's not just your perception: it's no accident that when you look at the consequences of various gaming "disorders," there's always a male and a female component:

for instance, with gaming addiction, suicide is more likely among women than men.

The change is real and tangible, because video games, even in their "bad" side, have actually always been kind of genderless, precisely because hiding behind a screen let a girl play online under a male nickname, and vice versa.

Today, the fact that video games aren't a male-only world anymore, like you said, is broadly accepted, but it'll really sink in for the generations after ours: and if you think about it, those extreme cases you mentioned are the toxic product of time and experience — a blog post or Reddit thread from a guy attacking women is unlikely to come from an eighteen-year-old, it's much more likely to come from someone in their thirties or forties.

After all, nobody's born racist or sexist, the problem creeps in as you grow up.

**That's pretty unsettling, but at least it gives us a legitimate reason to feel optimistic about newer generations, hoping they turn out better than us.**

**I think we've reached the last question, and you only get one answer! What's your all-time favorite game?**

I actually have two!

**I'll let you mention both, but for now you have to pick one!**

Ugh, that's hard! But ok, fine, as my one answer, talking about the game that really scratched the itch for me, I'll say *Halo*.

**Talk about breaking stereotypes about girls and gaming!**

I'm actually a pretty big scaredy-cat when it comes to video games, so I made a huge racket, and with *Halo* and the Elite hunters I properly experienced fear!

I also find it really fascinating how, in the more recent entries, you get to switch sides and experience the perspective of the human invading another planet, instead of the classic alien invader.

**Really fascinating, I agree! But let's indulge ourselves — go ahead and name the other game too.**

Well, *Final Fantasy*… how could anyone from our generation not say *Final Fantasy*?

**Which one exactly?**

Ok, I'll tell you: as I mentioned, I mostly watched other people play, and I personally played through two that are pretty much polar opposites, namely *Final Fantasy VII* (which is obviously my favorite) and *Final Fantasy XIII*, which was cute, sure, but they might as well have called it something else, it felt more like *Star Ocean* to me than a *Final Fantasy*...

**You have no idea how much I agree with that! A million thanks for everything, you've been incredibly valuable!**

Thank you!
