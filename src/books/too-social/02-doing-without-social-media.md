Let's think of social media in terms of a vice:

drinking alcohol, smoking, eating too much fried food and so on are all harmful vices (especially in excess) but at least they provide a more easily identifiable, and basically immediate, form of gratification:

no aimless, bored *scrolling*, no waiting for the endorphin hit that comes from yet another notification (which might even come from a bot) and, above all, **no gargantuan waste of time**.

## But I use social media to stay informed!

My dear imaginary friend, think about all the **clickbait** that's appeared before your eyes over the years, and really reflect on that claim.

Actually, consider this:

**do you really need a constant stream of updates on what's happening in the world?**

Are we really sure it isn't better to read a morning paper that's had the time to weigh the day's news (or at least one hopes so)?

The exception I'll partly grant concerns extraordinary events:

natural disasters, wars, attacks and so on... but even there, unless you or your loved ones are directly involved, is it really so important for you to know *everything, right away*?

Sure it's not just FOMO?

## Fo-what?! Is that some kind of disease?!

FOMO is one of those acronyms Americans love so much, and it stands for *Fear Of Missing Out.*

In short, the fear of being left out.

It's a feeling that's especially intense during adolescence, but which characterizes humanity broadly speaking.

## Got it, but FOMO aside, I also need social media for work…

That, unfortunately, is a whole different matter that deserves every bit of understanding.

Whether you're a social media manager working on behalf of others, or someone who needs to promote content, products, or creative work, I realize that hanging around those places is, for you, basically physiological — fundamentally necessary to bring home the bacon.

What you could do, though, is *limit* your use of social media to a work context — something that actually happens more often than you'd think.

After all, if social media is your job, they're probably not places you'd want to hang out in during your free time too — **in a certain sense it would be like living in your office.**

On this topic, I warmly recommend this great post by Kenobit: [*Strategies for Defending Your Time from Instagram*](https://log.livellosegreto.it/kenobit/strategie-per-la-difesa-del-tempo-da-instagram).

## Ok, but I also need social media to stay in touch with my friends.

Seriously?

I don't want to question that: but, from this point of view, even if they've become *very* "socialized," don't you think it's better to just use a simple messaging app?

## It's different: a status update in the timeline, a photo, or a story sparks a conversation I otherwise wouldn't know how to create out of nowhere.

Great point.

## Thanks!

You're welcome!

What you're saying really is a fundamental feature of the social web, and it goes way back — it was the golden age of themed forums in the late '90s and early 2000s:

topics and conversations (*topic*, *thread*) where friendships and socially fulfilling exchanges were born.

The big difference is that, generally, **these communities didn't have insanely rich companies or algorithms behind them treating their users as a statistic.**

Behind a lot of forums, there were people driven more by passion than by profit.

## Does anything like that exist today?

Various corners of the Fediverse [^1] come quite close to that philosophy, partly because there too we're not talking about a massive environment... which might actually be a strength!

From what I've experienced, it's easier to feel at home and bond in small communities / instances than with thousands of followers, of whom you end up seriously getting to know maybe fifty at most.

Concretely, **smaller numbers translate into far more manageable running costs and moderation challenges**.

## Isn't it a contradiction to attack commercial social media and praise this hippie Fediverse?

Partly, maybe, yes:

the point is that social media does have undeniable upsides, first of all connecting people who are far apart, who might share the same passions and want to communicate with like-minded people.

But if we think about it, **the commercial social media system by now is based relatively little on communicating, and mostly on *following***, which is a fairly different thing.

## Can you explain that better?

I'll try.

Pasolini, talking about television, once said:

> "Television is a mass medium, and a mass medium can only commodify and alienate us. \[...\] The moment someone watches you on screen, they have a relationship to us that runs from inferior to superior — a frighteningly undemocratic relationship." [^2]

essentially, **the same thing happens with content creators who have millions of followers, speaking to an audience that is theoretically on the same level (they're all users of the same platform), but in practice made up of spectators** in a position of inferiority dictated by that numerical (and consequently economic) status.

For that matter, we could think of commercial social media as the last mass medium of this historical phase, given how heavily it's used by different age groups.

## Well, but I *like* following what content creators put out!

Naturally, but I do think content creators themselves should think about what they're offering and **where and how they decide to put it out there**.

A platform like Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, or TikTok can freely decide to **restrict or even remove content (or whole accounts) at its own discretion**, while shifting off any responsibility as a mere "container" if something bad happens:

basically, *if the content works, it's theirs; if it causes problems, it's yours.*

![](/img/libri/too-social/file0.jpg)

> "Can I ask you something, counselor? When we're the ones doing the screwing, we're always in pairs, but when we're the ones getting screwed, we're always alone! Just asking, you know... so I know how to behave!" - Gigi Proietti

This is also why **it's wrong to talk about "censorship" when a social media platform decides to restrict an account or delete a post**:

it's not a matter of freedom of expression, because it's their toy, and they can legally do whatever they want with it.

## But what alternatives are there?

Simply put, **a return to the old-school websites** where authors actually own their own content and are free to present it however they like (within the limits of the law).

Right now, thinking along those lines is fairly utopian, because social platforms are the primary distribution channel for online marketing — the key to reaching an audience.

It's no accident that **many social platforms have always undermined the use of external links**, trying to keep users from ever leaving their own ecosystem.

Still, a few virtuous examples are starting to bloom.

## Like what?

[Owncast](https://owncast.online/) and [Peertube](https://joinpeertube.org/) are extremely customizable open alternatives that, thanks to the ActivityPub protocol, integrate perfectly with the aforementioned *hippie Fediverse*, as you call it.

## Fine, I can follow you on entertainment... up to a point. But how do I get information and news without social media?

In short?

*By looking for it.*

It might sound strange to anyone who started using and living the web with the arrival of the smartphone (we'll get back to that in a few pages), yet for years that's exactly how we went about it.

By the way, I'd like to stress **how important it is to search for the news *you actually want* instead of waiting for whatever the algorithm has decided to feed you.**

## And how do I keep up with what my friends / acquaintances are doing?

If you're particularly curious to know more about someone's life…

Well, honestly, there aren't many alternatives to social media. Except one.

## Which is?

*Contact the person.*

Also consider this:

if we take Facebook as an example, quite often the content isn't original but *shared*, so **a lot of people, digitally speaking, are nothing but distributors of other people's stuff** (more or less knowingly).

This isn't necessarily a bad thing, mind you, since sharing is something beautiful and lets you define your tastes and interests by showing them to others.

But maybe doing it by writing something in your own words, instead of recycling a meme you've already seen in seventy-five different contexts, could turn out to be more stimulating.

## But doing something like that is tiring, I work all day long and I don't feel like composing poetry when I talk to other people…

Fair enough.

But something authentic and personal doesn't necessarily have to be beautiful, heartfelt, creative, or polished: the point is that it's *yours*, something you feel the need to express in that particular moment.

In fact, the idea that you should have "performance anxiety" before posting something, for fear of not living up to the standard you see around you (especially on Instagram), is yet another sign of just how toxic and stifling social media can be.

## But if I share something that does well, people are more likely to notice me and interact with me. I don't want to post without ever getting likes or comments — I'd get depressed from missing that endorphin hit…

Aside from the fact that it's by no means guaranteed that something viral will catch the interest of your own circle (who, as we said, might've already seen that stuff a hundred times), you know what?

*Amen.*

**Writing on the internet should, first and foremost, meet your own need to communicate something**.

Of course, if it also catches other people's attention, great, all the better, because that's a beautiful thing... but it shouldn't be your ultimate goal.

Unless you're doing it for work, obviously (but we've already talked about that).

## But it's so nice to watch the like count go up…

Sure, because that's where ***gamification***[^3] comes into play, bringing with it a kind of *mathematical sublime* in watching yourself "level up" your interactions.

But think about it for a second: are you really sure it's nicer to have 500 anonymous likes than a single reply from someone you actually genuinely care about?

A reply that might spark a good discussion involving other people too, not a fan-war flame that's of no use to anyone…

Except, of course, to whoever runs the platform where the flame war is happening.

## Ok fine, but if everyone in the world is there, doesn't it feel like you're playing the antisocial nonconformist trying to look different?

Hmm, yes and no:

first of all, I wouldn't be so sure that *"everyone in the world is there,"* strange as that might sound.

I certainly don't want to downplay the influence and reach of social media, but we're talking about giant bubbles that are themselves divided into lots of smaller bubbles, often polluted by bots, inactive users, or outright fakes put there just to inflate that same numbers game we've already talked about.

Think about it for a second: do you think a poll about voting intentions on X matches the actual outcome at the ballot box?

Or that memes, slang and trends that spread on Instagram are just as popular down at your local sports bar?

We live in a profiled, deeply individualistic era, so much so that it's hard to still talk about "mass communication."

> the multiplication of supply, the diversification of the devices through which we access it, as well as the fragmentation of the audience, raise problems that are as much theoretical as methodological. Setting these aside, it is undeniable that we are witnessing today what has been called a demassification of mass communication (Chaffee, Metzger, 2001), one that calls into question media's ability to homogenize or offer a mainstream vision of the world [^4]

[^1]: the Fediverse is a group of decentralized social media platforms, joined together by the [ActivityPub](https://www.w3.org/TR/activitypub/) protocol. Right now, the most widespread and fairly well-known software is Mastodon, quite similar to the old Twitter.

[^2]: Pier Paolo Pasolini in Enzo Biagi's "III° B, roll call" (1971, Rai)

[^3]: gamification means borrowing game-like elements (levels, badges, leaderboards) in a context that isn't actually a game.

[^4]: Bentivegna S., Boccia Artieri G. (2019) Le teorie delle comunicazioni di massa e la sfida digitale, Laterza.
