If the last few years have taught us anything, it's that web communication keeps drifting further and further away from the written word:

images, memes, and above all video (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, etc.) call the shots:

it always comes back to dopamine, the famous shrinking attention span, and the perverse mechanism of algorithms able to *anticipate* what might catch our interest:

TikTok is very probably the most emblematic case, and the one that's pushed us toward a more passive, and if you like, more *TV-like* internet.

## But come on, Reddit is alive and well, and still mostly text-based!

Boards like Reddit are an exception, that's true, but they're also fertile ground for the infamous acronym *TL;DR* (*too long; didn't read*).

Nothing strange here either: the web is made up of constant cross-contaminations that shape user habits regardless of where they're hosted.

Coming back to the "visual" side of things, I'd say a lot of the responsibility lies with Facebook once again, which in 2005 rolled out unlimited image storage and gave the algorithm a certain *nudge* to favor visibility for images over text (not to mention external links).

## What about Instagram?

Launched in 2010 by Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger and bought by Facebook in 2012 for around a billion dollars, Instagram was certainly crucial in cementing the whole *image*-first approach, riding the wave of the smartphone boom already mentioned and the improvement of internet connections around the world.

Closely related is the story of *Stories*, invented by Snapchat in 2011 and quickly becoming a de facto standard across pretty much every major platform.

## And so we get to TikTok.

I'd say we're there, right?

By 2018 the timing was ripe, and after a whole string of events in China, ByteDance's Douyin (抖音) merged with Musical.ly, another Chinese app founded by Alex Zhu and Luyu Yang that ByteDance had acquired the year before.

ByteDance is an interesting outlier in our story, being the only big social media giant born outside the United States — and, not by coincidence, one that's run into more than a few problems because of it, like [the infamous ban of January 2025](https://apnews.com/article/supreme-court-tiktok-china-speech-national-security-22d672d946b6b4065ae5fb7f3e0d8bed) and President Trump's role in reversing it.

TikTok, being the newest arrival and the most popular among younger generations, probably takes that same passivity I mentioned earlier and pushes it even further.

## You also said *TV-like*, meaning we've all become spectators?

Yes, if you think about it, people who used to be labeled *lurkers* [^23] probably make up the majority today.

But even beyond active participation, just think about the generic consumption of content:

**these days, people search out news less and less, preferring to just wait for it to be served up directly to them.**

It's not for nothing that for years now we've seen constant friction between old-school online newsroom staff and the world of social media, culminating in the infamous *clickbait* from the papers themselves, which, to survive under algorithmic logic, keep inventing increasingly scandalous, screaming headlines.

It's a dynamic we've also seen at newsstands, but the social media context has taken it to an extreme:

## So it's all connected? In what sense?

Speaking hypothetically?

1.  Social media trains us for *hyper-dopaminergic* speed, with super-short images or videos;
2.  Attention spans drop;
3.  Information, like any complex piece of content, ends up passing only through screaming headlines, summaries, often inaccurate ones.

## And here too, is there anything else we can do besides quitting social media?

We could think in terms of legal or political battles, but… given how things are going?

I'd say no.

I often bring up one thing:

**I've never actually deleted my MySpace account, I just stopped using it**.

A good chunk of the world (not too big a chunk, let's be honest) **has stopped using Facebook for similar reasons**.

On top of that, for purely generational reasons, large parts of newer generations have never even created a Facebook profile.

Sure, **Meta is a frighteningly more powerful entity than MySpace ever was**, and recent years don't leave much room for overly optimistic scenarios about the future: I don't think it'll be easy to truly get rid of it.

Still, a few small antibodies are starting to show up, and even if it's a tiny minority, at least today there's a bit more awareness than before.

Awareness that, in my own small way, I hope this little book helps to spread.

[^23]: in old-school web slang, a lurker is someone who reads and follows an online community's activity without ever actually taking part themselves (basically, someone who just watches)
