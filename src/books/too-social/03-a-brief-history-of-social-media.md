## Social Media or Social Network?

Since these two terms keep popping up, for the sake of clarity let's briefly turn to professors **Andreas Kaplan and Michael Haenlein**: [^5]

by *Social Media* we mean web platforms where we can share and spread content, or rather *media* (a Latin word!) within the ecosystem that took shape starting with Web 2.0.

By *Social Network*, we're talking about a **social web that *can* be included within Social Media**, but which isn't actually defined by content (which may not even be present), but rather by exchange and interaction between users.

## My head hurts…

Just a little more and then I'll lighten things up, I promise!

Wikipedia tells us that the first Social Media platform in history was **SixDegrees**, by Andrew Weinreich.

Launched in 1997, SixDegrees allowed users to create profiles, friend lists, and, well, even if still in embryonic form, the elements that would go on to define the future Web 2.0.

As often happens with new ideas, SixDegrees arrived too early and ended up shutting down just three years later, at the start of the new millennium.

To be fair, we should remember that social communication on the web already had its precursors, with things like **Usenet** (1994), the various **BBSs** (*bulletin board services*, very popular on the early '90s internet) and, of course, the forums already mentioned.

All of it accompanied by blogs, chats, and various messaging software.

## Who?!

If that's your answer, I'm guessing you don't know / remember **MySpace**!

![](/img/libri/too-social/file1.jpg)

Tom Anderson, aka *"MySpace Tom,"* was among the founders of what we might call the first mainstream social network on an international scale, as well as the default first "friend" for anyone who signed up.

together with other eUniverse employees, Tom founded MySpace, inspired by its near-contemporary **Friendster** (2002), itself the first social network to reach 1 million members.

## Oh, MySpace… the one with all the popstars!

That's the one, exactly!

Officially launched in 2004, it was hugely important for launching international pop stars and for weaning off (or cementing) entire scenes far from the mainstream media, like regional rap scenes, Scandinavian metal, J-Pop, and so on.

In many ways, MySpace anticipated YouTube, but it was also a cross between the future Facebook and the old-school blogs, offering its users a shared platform while at the same time allowing a huge degree of personal customization:

through HTML and CSS code, users could give their page a look radically different from the "norm," so much so that a person's page could often be considered **an expressive part of the artist, exactly like a website**.

From this point of view, MySpace was perhaps the last representative of what I call "Web 1.5," though we could also consider it the first true standard-bearer of Web 2.0.

## Huh?

I'm sure you've already read or heard about these "versions" of the web (more or less knowingly).

*Web 2.0* is a term coined in 1999 by **Darcy DiNucci**, developer and author of the article *Fragmented Future*:

> "The Web we know now, which loads into a browser window in essentially static screenfuls, is only an embryo of the Web to come. The first glimmerings of Web 2.0 are beginning to appear, and we are just starting to see how that embryo might develop. The Web will be understood not as screenfuls of text and graphics but as a transport mechanism, the ether through which interactivity happens. It will \[...\] appear on your computer screen, \[...\]on your TV set \[...\] your car dashboard \[...\] your cell phone \[...\] hand-held game machines \[...\] maybe even your microwave oven." [^6]

Later, the term found real popularity in 2004, when publisher Tim O'Reilly launched the **Web 2.0 Summit**, a conference held annually until 2011.

## Ok, so basically it's a way to mark two eras of the web?

Yes, let's say we could simplify it that way.

Of course, these are fairly generic terms used to sum up a whole range of social and technological developments:

between the late '90s and the early 2000s, the internet ecosystem rapidly passed through entire historical eras, almost as if in the space of a couple of years we'd gone from the Renaissance to the Industrial Revolution.

That's due to the inherent speed that characterizes the web, as well as the growing influence of the tech sector on human society.

## What about Facebook?

Ah, here's the elephant in the room, right?

Say what you will, Facebook was the real *gamechanger*.

No other social media has ever reached similar numbers, and very few corners of the web have had a comparable influence on global society.

## Is it true it was born because Mark Zuckerberg got dumped by his girlfriend?

Someone's seen Fincher's *The Social Network*, huh?

Well, basically, yes.

Gossip aside, Mark Zuckerberg was a brilliant Harvard student — one of the most important universities in the world — and Harvard itself was the cultural soil where Facebook was able to sprout and thrive.

In short, Zuck was a programming prodigy who found himself with the right idea, at the right time, in the right place.

To get a sense of the guy, I'd recommend watching [his guest lecture at Harvard's CS50 on December 7, 2005](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xFFs9UgOAlE).

Mark was twenty-one at the time, and Facebook had launched not quite two years earlier, reaching, over the course of its very short life so far, **6 million active users**.

Listening back to it today, Mark sounds more like Elliot Alderson from *Mr Robot* than the extremely powerful, almost Orwellian billionaire he's now known as by the masses.

In fact, there's one trait that's true, more or less, of all the sacred monsters of big tech, and Mark himself points it out pretty well around the 21:40 mark:

> I think one of the interesting things about a lot of companies that end up being successful \[...\] is that they started with someone who was trying to build something great, not someone who was trying to build a company. Google came out of Larry and Sergey's PhD thesis at Stanford, and Yahoo came out of some Stanford guys who happened to be joking around in their dorm room. And eBay came from a guy who was trying to build a marketplace for his girlfriend to trade PEZ dispensers. Amazon was already a more calculated affair.

if we want to be a bit mischievous about it, it sounds a little like the bat-maxim from Nolan's *The Dark Knight*:

> You either die a hero, or you live long enough to see yourself become the villain.

Actually, there's another element that's so obvious it's rarely mentioned — one that I don't think Mark himself was even fully aware of.

Behind the digital giants that made Silicon Valley history, you'll typically find straight, white men belonging to the *upper bourgeois aristocracy* of the United States.

## Oh no, not the *woke* rant!

No, no, nothing like that: I'm just pointing out an objective fact.

In fact, as a European, I'd like to stress the US factor, echoing what Julian Assange once said:

> Facebook is totally centralized. Twitter is totally centralized. So is Google. They're all in the United States, all controllable by whoever holds coercive power [^7]

Of course, there are specific historical and geopolitical reasons that made the development of these hugely powerful companies natural in that particular setting — much like the reasons that made Florence the Silicon Valley of the 15th century.

What I'd mostly like to stress is **just how much the socio-cultural context of the United States shaped the handful of people who ended up influencing the entire globe.**

## Fine, but the US has been influencing the world since the end of World War II. Just think of cinema, television, music, to stay within entertainment…

Agreed, and I realize that going into detail on that would take us too far from the topic of this book:

the point is the sheer reach of these tools (think about how much they must have simplified intelligence work) relative to their geographic concentration: TikTok, from this point of view, is a real anomaly — but we'll get to that in a couple of chapters.

Back to Facebook: I could bring up plenty of controversial episodes about Zuckerberg's creation (now part of the Meta universe), but I'll just recommend [The Guardian's Facebook Files](https://www.theguardian.com/news/series/facebook-files) and mention **the infamous Cambridge Analytica affair**, where the profiling of accounts and use of the resulting data appears to have influenced the 2016 US election and Brexit.[^8]

## So basically, all this song and dance is just to say *Facebook is bad and ugly, back in my day this was all fields!*

Actually no, or at least not exactly.

I'll admit it:

I've had a *terrible* opinion of Facebook since its earliest days, but I find its origins, and young Zuckerberg's journey, genuinely fascinating — however brilliant he was, I doubt he fully realized at the time that he was about to become a revolutionary figure in communication.

In fact, I think the money and power that came from Facebook ended up cannibalizing that guy, who's now completely hollowed out and filled up by his own creation.

Obviously it's not the only such case, but this idea has been well explored by the [**Financial Times**](https://www.ft.com/content/20c42d38-f007-11e9-a55a-30afa498db1b):

> Worth \$70bn at age 35, why does Mark Zuckerberg keep working? Because work is who he is. [^9]

## Twitter!

Exactly! Although, as you probably know, these days it's more correct to call it **X**.

Launched in March 2006, and despite being inextricably linked to Jack Dorsey, **Twitter actually has much more "collective" roots than Facebook**.

Twitter, in fact, grew out of the experience of **TXTMob**, an activist project dating back to 2004 that was, in plain terms, a system for collectively managing and reading messages based on old-school SMS.

## Are you telling me Twitter is a child of radical activist collectives?!

In the American sense, sure, but more or less, yes.

There's a long article about exactly this on [Crimethinc](https://it.crimethinc.com/2022/10/28/the-billionaire-and-the-anarchists-tracing-twitter-from-its-roots-as-a-protest-tool-to-elon-musks-acquisition), if you're curious.

Here too, it's hard not to think of Sam Esmail's *Mr Robot*:

it's no coincidence that all that cultural backdrop heavily shaped the early 2000s, what we might call the golden age of *Hacktivism*.

2006 is also the year **WikiLeaks** was founded, and, over on 4chan, the now-famous **Anonymous** group began to take shape.

## So Twitter has always been politicized?

Certainly not in the way we might understand that today, given Elon Musk's role in the new Trump presidency… But more so than other general-purpose social media? Yeah, I'd say so.

Among other things, it's rather paradoxical how over the years **Twitter quickly transformed into the establishment's preferred communication channel:**

a platform born out of groups of activist misfits (not too different from those who animate today's Fediverse) soon became the go-to social network for journalists, politicians and, more broadly, leading figures on the global stage.

**Twitter was the *elite social network* well before its new owner, Elon Musk, ever showed up**.

And, partly for that reason, even though it never came close to Facebook's numbers (or later Instagram's and TikTok's), its resonance remains especially powerful.

Maybe too powerful, if you consider its actual usage.

## Ok, this list also has messaging apps and YouTube in it… can we really call all of that social media?

Great point: it's a complicated question, shaped by how the various platforms have evolved over the years.

In practice, these are environments where users interact, share media, and above all can build actual social *communities* (channels, groups, instances, etc.), so the label "social media" applies to them just fine too.

Coming back to Twitter, if you look at the numbers, I think it's clear that its influence has been massively overrated by the mass media *precisely* because it's traditionally been the platform of choice for journalists and public figures.

And obviously we could make a similar case for BlueSky (Dorsey's newest creation), Threads, or Mastodon.
