In our *brief history of social media*, we left off around the mid-2000s, the era corresponding to the explosion and rapid consolidation of social media.

And it's no coincidence that this same period saw the spread of the single most revolutionary piece of technology of our time:

**the smartphone.**

BlackBerrys, the LG Prada, but above all the first iPhone in 2007, laid the foundations for the web we know today — namely, **an internet that's browsed more on mobile than on PC**.[^17]

Not only that:

a huge share of the world's population, who up to that point had used a PC mainly for study or work, actually discovered the socially recreational side of the internet for the first time precisely with the rise of the smartphone.

## The arrival of the masses on the web, basically.

Precisely.

If up until then the internet had mostly been nerd and geek territory, or in any case populated by heavily digitalized people who had *grown up* alongside the network, the smartphone marked the arrival of a diverse wave of users of all ages and backgrounds.

The same wave that, besides porn, YouTube, and the odd Wikipedia page, **lived out its entire web experience almost completely within the borders of Facebook.**

## And then came the apps…

Well done!

This is another key point: **as smartphones proliferated, it became more and more common to access the web through "closed" apps rather than through a browser.**

On this note, I recommend this great article by From Jason: [Where have all the websites gone?](https://www.fromjason.xyz/p/notebook/where-have-all-the-websites-gone/).

The article also references this tweet by *cutestgoth*, which went viral in the winter of 2023:

![](/img/libri/too-social/file5.jpg)

as Jason explains nicely, **the web has increasingly become a place of passive consumption**.

Users are more and more inclined to use social media to *receive* ready-made content (be it videos, images, or news) and less driven to actively *search*, which is seen as a needlessly tiring exercise.

And it's no coincidence that lately the *dumbphone* trend seems to be timidly gaining ground, almost like a kind of antibody reacting to *brain rot* (the Oxford Dictionary's word of the year for 2024, which we might render as exactly that: brain rot).

## Hey, what's a *Dumbphone*?

Depending on your age, you might already know:

basically, a *non-smart* cell phone similar to the models made before 2007, and so before the iPhone and its friends showed up.

## Like the phones made for elderly people?

Basically, yes:

various Chinese brands and the historic Nokia have several in their catalog, inevitably playing on the nostalgia factor for Millennials and Gen X, who see in these technologically obsolete devices a classic fetish object from their youth.

It's basically the Obi-Wan Kenobi lightsaber effect: *An elegant weapon, for a more civilized age.*

And yes, if you take a look at various online shops, searching for dumbphones will pull up products with names like *Big-Button Cell Phone for Seniors*

## Well, obviously, among young people obsessed with Instagram and TikTok, who'd ever buy a piece of junk like that?

And here's an interesting paradox, mostly in the United States (other countries usually take a few extra years to catch up on this kind of thing):

plenty of young people who were born and raised with smartphones are consciously taking a step back to experience, for the first time, what life before hyper-connectivity was like.

For a deeper dive, I recommend reading the BBC's interesting article [*Not smart but clever? The return of 'dumbphones'*](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-60763168).[^18]

## I still see this as little more than yet another nostalgia-driven marketing move though — maybe even carried out through social media itself!

And you're right, no argument there:

the vintage appeal of these old gadgets isn't all that different from the one driving the boom in mini retro game consoles, the survival of vinyl records, or TV shows' obsession with '80s fashion and memorabilia.

That said, the pandemic years brought a lot of people to a fairly serious *internet overdose*, and consequently to a natural need to "detox," to recover more "analog" relationships.

## Ok, but isn't this the typical demonization of the new, the classic *"things were better when they were worse"*?

That element is definitely there, no denying it, but the smartphone conversation is quite different from the one we could have about any other household appliance:

> Our findings suggest that depression and anxiety are significantly linked with smartphone addiction. One national USA survey found that 46% of smartphone owners believed they could not live without their phones. [^19]

46% is a scary percentage, especially considering **this technology is barely twenty years old**, and that as a result we only have a handful of generations who've been born and raised with it so far.

## Is this where the famous *dopamine* story comes in?

Exactly.

It comes up pretty often these days, and it's a topic relevant both to social media as a whole and to the smartphone specifically:

dopamine is a neurotransmitter that governs how neurons in the brain communicate, so it's used in a whole range of vital functions, and in particular, it's released when we experience pleasurable sensations.

The classic mechanism at work here is *reward*: doing something that triggers a reaction which releases dopamine (shopping, food, notifications letting you know someone liked your post, and so on).

The smartphone, being constantly with us, amplifies the whole social media dynamic precisely because it guarantees a "dopamine hit" immediately and with relatively little effort, dramatically intensifying the dependency social media has built its business model on.

## But what can we do? I don't want, and can't afford, a *dumbphone* — too many things run through a smartphone these days!

Sadly true.

Society and governments, in a rather short-sighted way (or maybe out of self-interest), keep pushing harder and harder for the use of smartphones through apps, QR codes, payments, and account management that can't do without these gadgets anymore.

Personally, I'm considering the two-smartphone solution:

1.  The one I already have, dedicated to all the unavoidable stuff (banking, email, work, etc.), to be kept at home like a second PC;
2.  A very basic one, maybe not quite *dumb*, but with just enough to run Firefox, Telegram / WhatsApp, and Moshidon.

Obviously this isn't a choice that works for everyone, and it does mean giving up certain things, like good-quality photos (but hey, I'm no photographer, so amen!).

Generally speaking, there are no easy answers.

Still, I think becoming aware of the problem is the first step toward working out your own personal recipe:

Kenobit recently [published an essay](https://kenobitshop.bigcartel.com/product/liberare-il-mio-smartphone-per-liberare-me-stesso-3-fanzine-pillole-di-liberta-digitale) on exactly this topic, and in general has done a lot of work spreading the word about the Fediverse, which is a closely related subject.

## It seems like in everything you're saying, there's always a way-too-positive view of this "Fediverse." At the end of the day, doesn't it run on the same dynamics?

You're partly right, and I'd say it's time to talk in more depth about this blessed Fediverse of ours!
