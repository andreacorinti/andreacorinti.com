Do you know what's meant by an *echo chamber*?

## More or less: being in a context where you only hear your own voice echoed back?

In short, yeah, that's about it: [^12]

![](/img/libri/too-social/file4.jpg)

In practice, an echo chamber is a context in which people are exposed to information, and to other people, that tend to think the way they do, which reinforces their beliefs and generates a kind of tribal aggression when, for whatever reason, **they run into someone from another social bubble with different beliefs** (in other words, from another echo chamber).

Kurzgesagt summed up this concept really well in a great video: [The Internet is Worse Than Ever – Now What?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fuFlMtZmvY0)

## So basically, we're talking about a kind of eternal football derby

Exactly.

To sum it up, we could say that:

1.  the algorithm tends to connect you with topics and people you *might like*, to encourage you to spend your time on a platform that matches your tastes;
2.  the makeup of your bubble will logically gather users who resemble you, sharing beliefs and values you already hold;
3.  running into someone with radically different opinions from yours often leads to mockery, aggressive reactions, and sometimes to actual violence.

## But these things happened even before social media existed

Certainly, at least in part — it just takes a basic grounding in sociology to understand that *framing* is an issue as old as the world:

> When an individual is engaged in a framed activity, he retains some control over his immediate spontaneous involvement in it. This control can vary from boredom at one extreme (which may itself be a defensive response to an engrossing activity) to full absorption at the other. \[...\] When, for whatever reason, the individual breaks off the framed activity and realizes he has done so, the character of his engagement and belief changes suddenly. Whatever reservations he had about the activity are suddenly cut off, and, at least momentarily, he is likely to become intensely involved in his situation. [^13]

let's try to connect this idea to our current reality:

an individual believes a *fake news* story or some conspiracy theory, reinforced by the interactions they have within a bubble of other people who think the same way: all of this forms a *frame* that, once broken, can lead to a kind of identity crisis and correspondingly harmful reactions.

## Ok, but again: *fake news* and conspiracy theories weren't born with social media or the web, they're as old as humanity!

True, but **never before this historical period have they had the chance to take root and spread in such a widespread and polarizing way.**

The web doesn't have to answer to particular geographic limits, and from its very origins this has been the main strength of the *social network* concept:

a student in one city, a doctor in another, and a researcher in a third can freely interact and organize even if they're thousands of miles apart, often without regard to the beliefs and laws in force in their respective countries.

## Actually, that would explain why *flat-earth belief* has spread so much…

Exactly:

since the world of the web is global in the truest sense of the word, if we set aside language barriers or the forms of control adopted by individual nations (hi there, China!), monitoring and controlling the blossoming of "bubbles" that are more or less unhealthy for society becomes extremely difficult.

## And then there's anonymity! Anyone can hide behind a random nickname and picture!

Hold on, that's a different issue.

## But come on! If everyone used their real name on social media, they'd think twice before writing such nonsense!

Hm.

Do you really believe that?

Consider that Facebook has long had a similar policy, deleting tons of "fake accounts," *in theory* to improve the user experience, but in practice **to guarantee advertisers real, well-profiled targets.**

And do you really think insults and aggression don't show up even among people using Facebook under their real names?

## Ok, maybe not, but at least that way there's the option to report them!

Maybe.

Actually, you've just touched on something that was one of my biggest regrets while writing this book:

I never managed to interview a cybercrime police officer.

## So then?

So then, imagine for a second what would happen if *every* insult or act of aggression got reported to the proper authorities (which does happen, at least in part).

Now add in the possibility that the culprit lives in another country, that the victim doesn't have access to a serious legal team…

Quite the mess, don't you think?

## Ok, but then what should we do? Let people freely threaten and defame each other?

I'm not saying that, but we need to be pragmatic:

**the platforms we're talking about have *billions* of users and revenues comparable to the GDP of some countries.**

Setting aside individual responsibility, I think we should also think about the responsibility of the ecosystem where these things happen.

## But there is moderation, the option to report a post…

True, but do you know how it's actually handled?

Partly by algorithms, partly **by underpaid employees at third-party companies like Teleperformance, Foundever, or Concentrix.**

This highlights two things:

1.  Whoever runs social networks has little interest in actually moderating so-called *flame wars*, because they generate *engagement*, and therefore traffic / time spent on their services.
2.  Even if they genuinely wanted to be more virtuous about it, the sheer number of cases to handle is so frighteningly high that it probably couldn't be moderated even with proportionate investment.

And all this without even getting into Juvenal's eternal *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?*

## Huh?

If you know Alan Moore's comic *Watchmen*, you're probably more familiar with the English version, *Who watches the watchmen?*

In other words, *Who will guard the guards themselves?*

Once again, it's worth remembering that **the power of big digital companies now exceeds what most nations on the planet can exercise:**

> Big Tech companies are so profoundly different from other industries that they cannot be regulated the way trusts, cartels, or conglomerates of the past were. After all, if regulators tried to break up Facebook or TikTok, they'd find themselves facing furious users, for whom the universal nature of these platforms is exactly why they choose to use them. [^14]

## So what should we do, then?

For a start, **practice a more mindful use of these platforms.**

If one of your posts gets taken down by Facebook, if you can't monetize a YouTube video because of some far-fetched copyright interpretation, or you get banned from Instagram, that's not your freedom of expression being undermined.

Simply put, **it's not your toy**.

A social platform isn't a public service — it's the product of a private company, to which you're effectively handing over control of your entire online experience and all of its related content.

## Let's not exaggerate though, I have rights!

Sure, but the moment you decide to sign up for a social network, you're essentially signing a contract (the famous terms of service that literally no one ever reads).

Want a very topical example?

**Meta used more than a billion photos to train the AI behind its image generator.** [^15]

Chances are your own photos are somewhere in that billion. Did anyone ask for your consent to use them?

## …no?

Exactly: they don't need to, because *you already gave it* when you signed up.

## But that's not fair!

Legally speaking, it is; ethically, we can debate it… but, as you've probably gathered by now, I doubt that matters much to companies that are hard to negotiate with even "just" on tax matters. [^16]

The problem is that **a large part of the human species has been groomed and trained to *depend* on social media**, particularly the generations born and raised when it was already an established reality, but also (and maybe especially) all those so-called *boomers* who discovered the internet through Facebook.

This brings us to another topic crucial to the spread of the internet to the masses, and therefore fundamental to the success of social networks:

**the arrival of the *smartphone***.
